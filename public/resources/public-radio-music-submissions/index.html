How to submit music to public radio programs

“Public radio” is not one national submission desk. It is a network of independently programmed local stations, national distributors, and specific music programs. Independent artists gain traction by targeting the local or genre show that can genuinely use the release—not by pitching a network brand in the abstract.

How public radio programming works

A public station may carry nationally distributed news for most of the day while its local staff programs folk, jazz, classical, Triple A, or regional music blocks. Editorial authority sits with the producer, host, or local music director for that program.

Public-radio pitches benefit from more than a track: a strong regional story, cultural context, live-session readiness, or an articulate interview angle can make the release usable as programming.

Which releases are a realistic fit?

  • Jazz, classical, folk, roots, global, and Triple A releases with program fit
  • Artists with a truthful regional story or upcoming local performance
  • Projects with an interview, session, or cultural angle beyond a generic release notice
  • Professionally documented music with accurate credits and rights information

A station type is only the first filter. Confirm the format, recent playlist, service area, and individual show before collecting a contact. A relevant list of 40 prospects is a stronger campaign asset than 4,000 addresses selected only because they contain the word “radio.”

Find the right submission contact

Use the schedule to separate locally produced shows from national carriage. Pitch only the locally controlled program, and address the producer or host by their current role.

  1. Read the station's current music or submission page and follow it over any general advice.
  2. Check the schedule and recent playlists for the show or format that matches the release.
  3. Confirm the named person still holds the role on the station's own site.
  4. Record the source URL and date checked beside the contact, so your list can be maintained.

What to put in the pitch

Lead with program relevance and public value: regional connection, composition story, cultural context, or a timely live date. Keep the music link prominent and the story factual.

  • A specific subject line: artist, track, genre, release date
  • One sentence showing why this station or show is relevant
  • One stable streaming link, with a clean edit available where needed
  • Your city and any truthful local, tour, or audience connection
  • A direct ask to consider the track, followed by a polite sign-off

Use the radio pitch email templates as a framework, then rewrite the opening for every recipient.

A safe campaign workflow

Work in small waves. Research ten to twenty stations, send individual messages, then review replies and bounces before expanding. Suppress opt-outs immediately. Never scrape personal addresses, conceal the sender, or imply a prior relationship. Relevant one-to-one outreach protects your reputation and produces better learning than an indiscriminate blast.

Track interviews, session requests, playlist mentions, and archive pages as well as spins. A substantive local interview can create more discoverable value than a single unattended play.

Pre-send checklist

  • The station and show still operate, and the page was checked recently.
  • The release appears next to plausible peers in the current programming.
  • The recipient's role is relevant to music selection.
  • The email identifies you, contains an accurate subject, and offers an easy opt-out.
  • The campaign log has fields for sent date, response, follow-up, spin, and suppression.

Frequently asked questions

Do public radio stations accept independent music?

Some music programs do, while news-led schedules may have no relevant route. Research the local station and program; do not assume affiliation with a public network creates a central music policy.

Who should I contact at a public radio station?

Find the producer or host of the specific music program. A local music director or arts editor may be appropriate for broader coverage; follow any published form first.

How often should I follow up?

Follow up once, seven to ten days after the first message, unless the station publishes a different process. Do not add the address to an automated sequence. If there is no reply after one relevant follow-up, record the outcome and move on.
