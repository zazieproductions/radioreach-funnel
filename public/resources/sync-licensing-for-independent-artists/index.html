Sync licensing and film scoring for independent artists

Radio and sync are the two halves of the same sentence: someone with a slot to fill and a decision to make about your music. The difference is that a radio programmer needs a track that works between two songs, while a music supervisor or director needs a feeling that works under a scene. For independent artists writing instrumental, atmospheric, or experimental music — the artists who often find radio formats least hospitable — that second ask is frequently the more open door.

This guide covers how the sync side actually works: what to build, what to send, what the paperwork looks like, and how to start without an agent.

Why sync is a different business from airplay

Airplay promotes a recording; sync licenses it. Three consequences follow, and they change everything about how you prepare:

  • Nobody is listening for a hit. A supervisor is matching texture to picture. A cue that would be "too repetitive" for radio is often exactly right under a dialogue scene.
  • The terms are negotiable. Airplay is a yes-or-no. A license has a fee, a term, a territory, a media list, and a credit. You can say yes to a festival run and no to a national ad.
  • The buyer needs files, not vibes. Deliverables, metadata, and a clean rights chain decide whether a deal closes on Friday or dies in an inbox.

Two paths: license a finished track, or score to picture

Independent artists usually end up doing both, but they are different jobs:

  • Licensing an existing recording — the track already exists, a production wants to use it, and you grant rights. The work is administrative: rights confirmation, a short agreement, stems and instrumentals on request.
  • Scoring to picture — the music is made for the scene, to a locked cut, to a temp track that the director has already fallen in love with. The work is collaborative and iterative: spotting sessions, revisions, and versions timed to the frame.

If you have never scored, start with licensing what you already own. It is a real placement, it puts a credit on your page, and it teaches you the paperwork in a low-stakes way.

Build a cue library, not just a track list

A "cue" is a short, self-contained piece of music written for a specific job — tension, dread, drift, release. Supervisors and editors search by function, not by title, so a catalog organized as songs with three-minute structures is hard to use. Build cues instead:

  • Short and loopable. 30 seconds to two minutes, with clean edit points at bar lines.
  • Named for what they do. "Slow dread, low strings" beats "Untitled 4" every time.
  • Delivered in versions. Full, underscore (thin), no-percussion, and a 15-second button — four files per cue is normal.
  • Registered. Every cue gets a title, an ISRC, and a writer split before anyone licenses it.

Twenty good cues beat a hundred songs. Twenty is also enough to fill a page a director can browse in three minutes.

What a scoring portfolio has to prove

A director or producer evaluating an unknown composer is asking four silent questions: does this sound like the film, can this person hit a deadline, do they understand picture, and are they easy to work with. A portfolio that answers those questions out loud gets far more inquiries than a homepage full of moody photography.

Practically, that means: cues grouped by emotional register rather than by album; a short reel cut to picture (not a slideshow of stills); a credits list with the format and year of each project; and an inquiry path that asks the producer for the things you actually need to quote — format, scored minutes, and a deadline. One public example of that shape is the Zazie Productions horror and art-house score archive, where cues are filed by emotional register — atmosphere, dissonance, psychological tension — beside the film credits and a quote request that asks for scored minutes and picture lock. It is worth a look purely as a layout to borrow from.

Deliverables and metadata close deals

The unglamorous layer is where independent placements are won and lost:

  • Rights confirmation. You must control the master and the writer's share — or know exactly who does. A sample-free version of every pitch-ready track is the fastest way to stay safe.
  • Stems. Dialogue-safe and mixable. If a supervisor has to ask twice, you have lost momentum.
  • One-page agreement. Fee, term, territory, media, credit, and who handles cue sheet submission. Keep a template ready and never license on a handshake and a DM.
  • Cue sheet data. Title, writers, publisher, duration, and usage — so the back-end royalties actually reach you when the film airs.

Have this folder assembled before anyone asks. The composer who replies within an hour with a named file and a rate is the composer who gets the second film.

Where to pitch when you have no agent

  • Filmmakers you already know. Film school students, local directors, and editors you have worked with are the warmest possible leads — and their early features become your credits.
  • Film festivals and markets. Festivals are where directors and producers are briefly in one place and unusually willing to talk about their next project.
  • Music libraries. Non-exclusive libraries are a legitimate volume play. Read the reversion clause and the re-titling terms before you sign anything exclusive.
  • Micro-budget and genre productions. Horror, thriller, and documentary are consistently the most music-hungry corners of independent film, and the ones most open to a composer with a distinct voice.
  • Direct outreach with a specific ask. Send one cue that matches the project, a two-line note about the film you just watched, and a link. Not a catalog dump.

How radio and sync feed each other

These channels are not rivals. A confirmed airplay list is credibility on a composer inquiry; a festival credit is a fact a radio host can read into a microphone. Artists working both sides get to reuse every win twice:

  • Use your EPK and one-sheet as the base document, then add a cue page for the film audience.
  • When a placement lands, treat it like a spin: add it to the sheet, post it, and cite it in the next radio pitch.
  • Keep the do-it-yourself economics honest — the same trade-off applies to whether you pitch supervisors yourself or pay an agent.

Your first 30 days

  1. Week 1: Cut twenty existing tracks into cues, name them for function, and export the four standard versions of each.
  2. Week 2: Assemble the folder — one-page agreement, rights list, stems, ISRCs, writer splits.
  3. Week 3: Publish a single page for the catalog, grouped by emotional register, with a hook to listen and a form that asks for format, scored minutes, and deadline.
  4. Week 4: Pitch ten specific filmmakers or productions with one matched cue each — and keep the radio campaign running in parallel.

Both channels reward the same behavior: a catalog that is easy to use, and a reply that arrives fast with everything attached.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a publisher or sync agent to license my music?

No — plenty of independent placements happen directly between an artist and a filmmaker or a small production company. What you do need is clean paperwork: a one-page license agreement, confirmation that you control both the master and the publishing share, and a walk-away price. Agents and libraries earn their percentage by bringing repeat buyers and handling the back-and-forth, which matters more once you have a catalog worth representing.

What does a sync license usually pay an independent artist?

For independent film, student projects, and small-brand work, fees commonly land anywhere from a few hundred dollars to a few thousand for a festival-and-festival-only license — and plenty of micro-budget placements pay little or nothing in cash for the exposure and the credit. Broadcast, national advertising, and theatrical releases sit in a different range entirely. Price the term, territory, and media you are actually granting, and put an expiration on any discount.

Can I license a song that samples another record?

Not cleanly. A sample requires clearance from both the master owner and the publisher of the underlying composition, and most music supervisors will not run that risk on a tight schedule. Keep a sample-free version of any track you intend to pitch for sync — it doubles as the safest thing to send when a supervisor asks for an alternate.

Does sync work replace radio promotion?

No, they do different jobs. Radio builds a story in a market and puts your name in front of listeners; sync puts your recording into a scene that travels to festivals, streaming, and broadcast. Artists who do both can cite one in the pitch for the other — airplay is credibility on a one-sheet, and a placement is a fact a radio host can read into a microphone.
