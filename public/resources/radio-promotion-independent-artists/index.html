Radio promotion for independent artists

Independent radio promotion works best when it is treated as research and relationship-building rather than a giant email blast. The goal is not to contact every station. It is to identify stations where your music plausibly belongs, and make it easy for the relevant programmer to hear it, add it, and remember you the next time you release something.

Build the right radio contact list

A useful radio contact list combines station information with enough context to decide whether outreach makes sense. Depending on the station, useful contacts may include music directors, program directors, specialty-show hosts, DJs, and general submission addresses.

Contact quantity matters much less when the list has no relationship to your genre or audience. Start with relevance, then expand: fifty well-chosen stations will outperform five thousand scraped addresses every time.

Pitch music directors and programmers like humans

Programmers receive a large amount of promotional material. A short, specific pitch is easier to evaluate than exaggerated marketing language. State who you are, what the release is, why it might fit, and where the recipient can hear it.

Avoid repeated unsolicited messages, misleading subject lines, and giant attachments. Good radio promotion should look like professional correspondence, not automated spam.

Use geography strategically

Local and regional angles can create legitimate reasons for outreach. An artist's hometown, tour dates, local collaborations, events, and listener base all make a release more relevant to a particular market. That is different from manufacturing hundreds of nearly identical location pages or emails.

City-by-city research also makes campaigns easier to organize and measure: when a station in a market you targeted plays you, you can connect the airplay to the dates you pitched.

A realistic eight-week campaign timeline

Radio rewards structure. Here is a schedule that fits how stations actually process music:

  • Weeks 1–2 (pre-release): finalize your target list, verify contacts, prepare your one-sheet and streaming links, and send the first wave of pitches.
  • Weeks 3–4 (release): release week pushes — send wave two, post publicly, and nudge stations that opened your first email.
  • Weeks 5–6: polite single follow-ups, check playlists and charts for adds and spins, and thank stations that played you.
  • Weeks 7–8: compile results, update your list with what you learned, and log which contacts replied for next release.

Then stop. A campaign with a defined end protects your reputation and gives you a clean dataset for the next one.

Measure what actually happened

Radio results are quieter than playlist results, but they are measurable:

  • Adds and spins: search station playlists and NACC-related charts for your artist name weekly.
  • Market effects: watch streaming and traffic for bumps that line up with the stations and dates you pitched.
  • Relationships: a music director who replied once is an asset for your next release. Keep those names.

Frequently asked questions

How long should an independent radio campaign run?

Plan for six to eight active weeks around a release: two or more before release date for early adds, then four to six weeks of outreach, follow-ups, and chart watching. Radio moves in weekly programming cycles, so a campaign shorter than a month rarely gives stations time to add you.

How do I measure whether radio promotion worked?

Track three things: confirmed adds and spins (station playlists and charts are public evidence), traffic and streaming jumps that line up with airplay dates and markets, and the relationships you built for the next release. vanity impressions are not a metric — stations that played you twice are.

Should I promote one song or a whole album to radio?

Lead with one song. A single focus track makes it easy for a music director to add, chart, and report your music. Mention the album as context, but give programmers one clear thing to play.

Can I do radio promotion without a label?

Yes — that is exactly what this guide describes. What a label adds is manpower and relationships. An independent artist with a organized contact list, a tight pitch, and consistent follow-up can run a credible campaign alone.
