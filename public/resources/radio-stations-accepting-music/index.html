How to find radio stations accepting music submissions

Searching for radio stations accepting music is less about finding one universal submission directory and more about learning to recognize the stations, programs, and people that are open to independent releases. Submission policies change, and even stations that accept music may only consider releases that fit their programming. This guide covers how to read those signals before you spend an afternoon writing pitches.

Start with station fit, not email volume

Before collecting addresses, narrow the field by genre, geography, format, audience, and programming. A smaller list of plausible stations is usually more useful than thousands of unrelated contacts.

Look at recently played artists, specialty shows, local-music programs, station charts, and playlist logs. Those signals tell you whether a station is worth contacting — and give you the personalization material for a pitch that does not read like a template.

Five signals a station is open to submissions

You can usually tell how receptive a station is before you ever hit send:

  • A published submission page or email address. The strongest signal there is. Follow its instructions exactly.
  • Specialty shows in your genre. A two-hour weekly metal, jazz, electronic, or hip-hop program is programmed by a human who needs a steady supply of new music.
  • Visible playlist logs. Stations that publish recent spins — especially with independent artists in them — are actively playing music like yours.
  • Local and regional music programs. Shows dedicated to hometown artists exist specifically to receive your pitch.
  • Chart reporting. Stations that report to college and community charts are structurally set up to receive and process new releases.

Conversely, a station with no submission information, no playlists, and a strictly formatted commercial schedule is telling you something too. Respect the signal and move on.

Which radio contacts matter?

Useful contacts can include music directors, program directors, genre directors, DJs, specialty-show producers, and dedicated submission addresses. The right person depends on how the station is organized.

A music director may screen new releases at one station, while an individual DJ chooses music independently at another. That is why contact research and station research belong together: the best address in the world is useless if it belongs to someone who stopped booking music two years ago.

What should a radio submission email contain?

Keep the first message easy to process. Identify yourself, explain briefly what you are sending, provide a streaming link, and give the recipient a reason the release could fit the station. Include essential release information without turning the email into a biography.

Personalized does not have to mean long. One accurate sentence demonstrating that you know what the station plays can be more persuasive than several paragraphs of generic promotion. For full templates, see our radio pitch email templates.

A 60-second pre-send checklist

  • The station actually plays this genre (you checked a playlist, not a guess).
  • You are writing to a current, relevant human — or the station's stated submission address.
  • The subject line names the artist, the release, and the genre.
  • There is one clear streaming link, not five.
  • No attachments unless the station asked for them.
  • You have a note of where and when you sent it, for exactly one polite follow-up later.

Build a repeatable radio outreach workflow

Keep records of the station, contact, submission date, response, and any follow-up. This prevents duplicate outreach and lets you learn which types of stations respond to your music.

Frequently asked questions

Do internet radio stations accept music submissions?

Many do, and they are often more accessible than FM stations because programming is curated show-by-show. Treat them like any other target: find the show that fits your genre, identify the host or curator, and pitch with the same care you would give a broadcast station.

How many stations should I contact?

Quality beats volume. A list of 40–80 well-matched stations with correct contacts will usually outperform thousands of unverified addresses. Start where your genre genuinely fits, then expand as you learn which types of stations respond.

Do commercial radio stations accept unsolicited music?

Rarely. Most large commercial stations get music through label distribution, promoters, and chart-driven playlists. Independent artists usually earn better results from college, community, noncommercial, and internet radio — then use that airplay as evidence when approaching bigger targets.

Is it spam to email radio stations?

Targeted, relevant, personalized outreach is normal business correspondence. Sending identical mass emails to addresses you have never checked is spam — to programmers and to anti-spam laws alike. One personalized message plus one polite follow-up per station is a defensible, effective standard.
