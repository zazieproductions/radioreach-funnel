Radio promotion FAQ

By the RadioReach team Updated August 18, 2026 1 min read Reference & answers

These are the questions independent artists ask most about radio promotion — answered directly, with links to the full guides when you want depth. Nothing here requires a label, a promoter, or a budget beyond your own time.

Frequently asked questions

How do I submit my music to radio stations?

Find stations that program your genre, identify the right contact — usually a music director, genre director, or show host — and send a short personalized email with one streaming link and a one-sheet. Following the station's published submission process, where one exists, is the single biggest correctness point. Our step-by-step guide walks through the whole sequence.

How much does radio promotion cost?

Doing it yourself costs time: roughly 30–60 hours across an eight-week campaign, plus whatever you spend on contact research. Hiring an independent promoter for a college/noncommercial single campaign commonly runs from the high hundreds to low thousands of dollars. The DIY-versus-promoter comparison covers the trade-offs honestly.

How long does a radio campaign take?

Plan on six to eight active weeks per release: pre-release pitching, release-week pushes, follow-ups, and chart watching. Radio works in weekly programming cycles, so shorter bursts rarely give stations time to review, add, and report your music.

Will radio stations pay me when they play my song?

Not directly, in most cases. In the United States, terrestrial radio pays songwriting performance royalties to writers through their PRO (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC, GMR) — so register your songs and your performances with a PRO. Unlike most countries, US terrestrial radio does not generally pay performers or labels for broadcasting a sound recording. Registering the song is what matters.

Can I pitch radio before my song is officially released?

Yes — and for chart-oriented campaigns it is standard. Music directors often want music two to four weeks ahead of release date so they can review it and add it the week it is public. Just make sure a private stream is ready before you pitch.

How many stations should I pitch?

Quality over volume: a first campaign of 40–80 well-matched stations with correct, current contacts will beat thousands of unverified addresses. Expand as you learn which station types respond to your music.

What results should I realistically expect?

Modest percentages — a handful of adds and a scatter of spins is a genuinely good first campaign, and each confirmed spin is evidence for the next release. Beware anyone promising specific results; legitimate promotion reports what happened, never what will.

Is emailing stations I don't know spam?

Targeted, personalized, relevant outreach is standard industry practice. Identical mass emails to unchecked addresses are spam — reputationally and legally. One personalized pitch plus one polite follow-up per station is the professional standard our templates are built around.

Do I need to be on a label to get radio play?

No. College, community, noncommercial, and internet radio add independent releases every week. What labels provide is manpower and relationships — both things an independent artist can build with organization and repeated campaigns.

What's an add, and why does everyone talk about them?

An add is a station's decision to put your song into rotation. Adds are the visible milestones of a campaign because they are public, countable, and — at reporting stations — feed the weekly charts other programmers watch. The glossary covers adds, spins, rotation, and chart reporting in detail.

Should I hire a radio promoter or do it myself?

DIY wins for first releases, local scenes, and niche genres where the station map is small enough to work by hand. Hiring wins for chart goals, tour support, and releases already showing traction. Many artists land on a hybrid: DIY relationships at favorite stations, professional help for defined campaigns.

How do I know if a station actually played my song?

Check the station's playlist logs and any charts it reports to, searching your artist name weekly during the campaign. Confirmed spins are also worth a short thank-you email — the most underused move in radio promotion.

What materials do I need before pitching?

One streaming link that doesn't expire, a clean radio edit if there is profanity, a one-page one-sheet, a 100-word bio, and a working contact email. Our EPK guide covers the full kit stations expect.
