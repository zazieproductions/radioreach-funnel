DIY radio promotion vs. hiring a radio promoter

By the RadioReach team Updated August 18, 2026 3 min read Comparisons & strategy

Every independent artist reaches the same fork: spend the next six weeks pitching radio yourself, or pay someone with relationships to do it for you. Both paths are legitimate, and both get mis-sold. This guide lays out the real differences — cost, control, reporting, and outcomes — so you can choose based on your goals rather than your guilt.

What each path actually involves

DIY promotion means you research stations, verify contacts, write pitches, follow up, track adds, and build the relationships that compound across releases. It costs time — realistically 30 to 60 focused hours across an eight-week campaign — and almost no money.

Hiring a promoter means paying an independent radio promotion company or plugger to work your single to their station network for a defined number of weeks. They pitch, follow up, chase adds, and send you weekly reports. You stay responsible for the music, the assets, and the expectations.

When DIY wins

  • First or second release, when the goal is learning which stations fit you
  • Strong local and regional scenes, where your geography is the pitch
  • Niche genres with a small, reachable set of specialty shows — a promoter's network adds less when the whole map is 80 stations
  • Every release after this one: DIY relationships compound; you are building an asset

When hiring wins

  • Chart objectives: a serious NACC or noncommercial chart run needs sustained weekly pressure most artists can't schedule around a day job
  • Tour support: airplay in the markets you're about to play, timed to dates
  • Proven music: a single already showing traction deserves professional amplification
  • No time: the honest reason. A promoter doing the work beats you not doing it

Red flags when evaluating promoters

  • Guaranteed spins or chart positions — nobody honest guarantees programming decisions
  • No weekly reporting in the proposal — reporting is the product
  • Won't share example reports or past campaigns in your genre
  • Wants to pitch formats that don't match the music just to inflate the station count
  • Vague about pay-for-play arrangements — undisclosed paid spins are a payola problem for everyone involved, including you

The hybrid most artists land on

Run your first campaign or two yourself — with an organized contact list and tight templates — and keep notes on which stations respond. If a release starts moving and the next one deserves a bigger push, hire a promoter for that specific campaign and hand them your history: your add list, your contacts, your station relationships. You'll pay for reach and keep the knowledge.

Frequently asked questions

What does a radio promoter actually do?

A promoter pitches your release to their network of programmers, chases adds, reports weekly on spins and charts, and lends their reputation to your record. They do not — and cannot honestly — guarantee airplay. What you're buying is relationships and time: theirs instead of yours.

How much does an independent radio promoter cost?

For college and noncommercial campaigns, promoters commonly charge in the high hundreds to low thousands of dollars for a single running several weeks; bigger commercial-format campaigns cost multiples of that. Prices vary widely by genre, format, and campaign length — always ask what reporting is included.

Can I split the work — promote some myself and hire help too?

Yes, and it's often the smartest structure: keep the relationship-building at your local and genre-favorite stations yourself, and hand a defined campaign (a single, a chart run, a tour window) to a promoter. Agree in writing on who contacts whom so you never double-pitch a station.

Is promising guaranteed spins a scam?

Treat it as one. Legitimate promoters pitch and report; they don't pre-ordain results. Stations that play music in exchange for undisclosed payment run into payola rules — and an artist who pays for it inherits the problem.
