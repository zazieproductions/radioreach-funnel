# 3.2 The 90-Word Pitch — The Only Email That Matters

> **THE ONE-LINE TRUTH:** A PD reads your email for four seconds. The 90-word pitch is a weapon built for that window — hook, proof, fit, ask — no filler, no begging, no death.

## VIDEO SCRIPT (ready to record · runtime ≈ 09:20)

**[0:00 – 0:50] COLD OPEN — face to camera, tight.**
"Here's what a PD sees when they open a typical artist email: 400 words. Bio. Influences. Seven links. A press kit attachment. A request at the bottom. Four seconds of reading time. They read maybe two lines — and they're gone. Death by essay."

**[0:50 – 2:20] THE 90-WORD LAW — on-screen: "90 WORDS. 4 SECONDS. 1 ASK."**
"The 90-word pitch is the only email structure that respects the PD's actual attention budget. Ninety words is roughly four seconds of read. It forces you to decide what matters. If it's not in the 90 words, it doesn't matter — it goes in the one-page EPK (linked, not attached), where the curious PD can go deeper. The pitch is the doorway, not the house."

**[2:20 – 4:00] THE FIVE-BLOCK STRUCTURE — screen-share the template.**
"Block one, The Name: 'I'm [artist], 24, from [city].' Ten words. Block two, The Proof: one receipt — streams, spins, tickets, press. Twenty words, one sentence. Block three, The Fit: why this station — the format, the show, the local angle. Twenty-five words. Block four, The Ask: one clear request — 'Could [single] land in [show]?' Twenty words. Block five, The Logistics: link, 90-second audio, clean edit, phone. Fifteen words. Total: 90. One sentence per block. Nothing else."

**[4:00 – 5:50] THE DISSECT — the before/after demo.**
"Here's the 400-word version: 'Dear program director, I hope this email finds you well. I am an artist who has been working on my craft...' — death. Here's the 90-word version: 'I'm [artist], from [city]. 2,100 local streams this month, 450 tickets sold for [venue] on the 14th. [Single] is a 3:12 radio edit that sits in your [format] rotation. Would it fit [show/block]? Link, audio, clean edit — 60 seconds.' Read them both. Feel the difference. One asks. The other *is* the answer."

**[5:50 – 7:20] THE REWRITE MACHINE — the editing rules.**
"Rule one: kill every adjective you can't prove. Rule two: kill 'I hope,' 'just,' 'maybe,' 'please.' Rule three: kill your influences — nobody at the station cares who you sound like. Rule four: one link in the body, one attachment, never a folder. Rule five: end with a question they can answer with a yes. Six revisions minimum. The 90 words are not a draft — they're a final cut."

**[7:20 – 8:20] THE WORD-COUNT DISCIPLINE — 90, not 91.**
"Does 91 words kill it? No. But 140 does. The discipline is the point: if a sentence can survive being cut, it wasn't earning its place. Paste your pitch into a word counter. If it's over 90 — cut. If it's under 60 — you're hiding. 90 is the sweet spot: enough to prove, tight enough to read."

**[8:20 – 9:20] CLOSE — module card.**
"Now the proof behind the words: the one-page EPK that commands attention before they even click. Next lesson, we build the fortress."

## IMPLEMENTATION DRILLS

- **Drill 1 — The Effort Kill (30 min).** Delete your current pitch and write the 90-word version using `Templates/T3.2-90-Word-Pitch.md`. **Pass standard:** 85–95 words, five blocks intact, zero begging words.
- **Drill 2 — The Six-Revision Pass (45 min).** Revise it six times: kill adjectives, kill influences, cut sentences, tighten proof, sharpen ask, final read-aloud. **Pass standard:** the read-aloud takes under 30 seconds and you didn't stumble on the ask.
- **Drill 3 — The Station Variant (30 min per station).** For your top 5 war-room stations, rewrite only the Fit and Proof blocks. Keep name, ask, logistics identical. **Pass standard:** 5 personalized 90-word pitches, each referencing that station's actual format or show.
- **Drill 4 — The 30-Second Read-Aloud Test (10 min).** Record yourself reading the pitch at PD speed. **Pass standard:** the recording is under 30 seconds and sounds like a professional leaving a voicemail, not a fan sending a prayer.

## WAR-ROOM NOTES

**WAR-ROOM NOTES — 3.2 The 90-Word Pitch**

**WHY 90 WORDS IS A WEAPON, NOT A FORMAT (ATTENTION ECONOMICS)**

The PD's attention budget decays by the second. The first two lines decide open-or-scroll; the first 20 words decide read-or-skim; the first 90 decide reply-or-archive. An email over ~120 words requires the PD to *opt in* to reading — which virtually no stranger does. The 90-word structure is not artistic restraint; it's a tribute to the attention economy. You cannot win a battle in a 4-second window with a 400-word weapon.

**THE PROOF-FIRST REORDER (HIDDEN TRICK)**

Most artists open with their name and their music. The operator opens with *the market's proof* when it exists: "2,100 local streams this month" beats "I'm an artist" because it answers the risk question (Lesson 1.2) before the identity question. Only when your proof is weak do you lead with the human angle. But here's the unlock: the proof block is *the* block to personalize per station — the same pitch, different receipt, targeted to that market's data. Personalization ≠ flattery. Personalization = relevance.

**THE ONE-ASK PRINCIPLE (NEVER VIOLATE)**

A 90-word pitch with two asks ("play my song and check my Instagram and come to my show") is a zero-ask pitch. One ask, one answer, one next step. If the conversation advances, you get the second ask on the call (Module 5.1). Radio PDs are not deals to close in one email — they're customers to serve across 90 days.

**THE ATTACHMENT TRAP**

Never attach a folder, a 12-page press kit, or a video. Attach at most: one radio edit, one 90-second audio file, and a one-page EPK URL. If your EPK is a PDF that requires a download to evaluate, it's already failed. The one-page EPK (Lesson 3.3) is built to be a link — instant, mobile, gorgeous.

**THE FOUR-SECONDS TEST (PERSONAL LITMUS)**

Every launch, print your pitch, hold it at arm's length, and read it in four seconds. If you can't name the ask afterward, rewrite. This test catches 90% of the fluff that survives six revisions. It's brutal — and it's the standard.
