# 6.3 The Release Calendar That Dominates Quarters

> **THE ONE-LINE TRUTH:** A release is not an event — it's a 12-week operation. The artists who own radio don't drop singles; they run a quarterly release calendar with 3 campaigns a year, armed 8 weeks out.

## VIDEO SCRIPT (ready to record · runtime ≈ 09:10)

**[0:00 – 0:50] COLD OPEN — face to camera, a quarterly wall calendar.**
"Here's how most artists release: finish the song, post it, hope. One week of chaos, two weeks of silence, six months of shame. Here's how operators release: a calendar. Three releases a year. Eight weeks of preparation per release. Every station armed before the song even drops. That's the difference between a hobby project and a quarterly operation."

**[0:50 – 2:20] THE 12-WEEK RELEASE OPERATION — on-screen: the countdown.**
"Week 1–2: the proof build — collect receipts, refresh the stack, update the EPK. Week 3–4: the target arm — run the format scores, build the new 20-station war room for *this* release. Week 5–6: the weapon build — 90-word pitch, 90-sec audio, EPK, pre-roll asset. Week 7–8: the load sequence — seven gates, no exceptions. Week 9: the pre-roll — tour dates announced (2.2). Week 10: the launch — wave one and wave two to the armed 20. Week 11: the follow-up — touch 2 and touch 3 per the system. Week 12: the harvest — cascade, thank-you, proof drops, revenue lanes. Twelve weeks. Every release. The calendar is dominant because the *preparation* is dominant."

**[2:20 – 4:00] THREE RELEASES A YEAR — WHY NOT FOUR OR SIX.**
"Three releases a year is the production ceiling for a campaign that's actually armed. Four or six releases means one release gets a shallow run — and a shallow run is worse than no release, because it teaches the market that your singles don't stick. Three releases guarantees each one gets the full 12-week operation: full target arm, full load, full follow-up, full harvest. A release calendar isn't creative — it's capacity planning. Three disciplined campaigns beat six half-hearted ones."

**[4:00 – 5:30] THE QUARTERLY RHYTHM — one release per quarter, offset.**
"Q1: the first release. Q2: the follow-up + summer dates. Q3: the breakout attempt — best song, biggest campaign. Q4: the harvest + the annual audit. Offset the calendar from the industry: January (post-holiday open), April (spring), September (fall). Right before the December radio silence — nothing new in November or December, because PDs are in ratings season. The quarterly rhythm means your name is always in rotation in *somebody's* market, and every quarter's adds feed the next quarter's proof."

**[5:30 – 7:00] THE CALENDAR AS A WEAPON — screen-share template T6.3.**
"Open the release calendar. Columns: release date, W-12 to W-0 checkpoints, targets armed, campaign window, harvest window, revenue lanes. Every checkpoint has a due date. Every due date has an owner. If the calendar is blank, the campaign will be a fire drill. The calendar is not a nice-to-have — it's the contract between you and your future self. Sign it."

**[7:00 – 8:10] THE PROTECTED BLOCKS — the operator's week.**
"Inside a 12-week operation: 20 minutes a day war room. 90 minutes a week content/proof. 2 hours a week targeting. 1 day a month audit. That's ~6 hours a week on the machine — and it runs the entire year. The calendar protects those blocks: each release's W-10 to W-2 is sacred. You don't cancel the campaign because life happens. You structure life around the calendar. That's what operators do."

**[8:10 – 9:10] CLOSE — module card.**

**WAR-ROOM NOTES — 6.3 The Release Calendar That Dominates Quarters**

**CAPACITY PLANNING, NOT CREATIVITY (THE OPERATOR'S FRAME)**

The dominant constraint on radio success is *armed campaigns*, not musical output. Each armed campaign requires roughly 40 hours of machine time: targeting (6h), weapons (8h), load (4h), sends (3h), follow-up (5h), proof farms (10h), audit (4h). At three releases a year, that's ~120 hours — sustainable. At six releases, the per-release hours collapse below the armed threshold, and each release underperforms its *own* potential. The calendar's "three a year" is a math decision, not an artistic taste decision.

**THE W-8 GATE (THE MOST VIOLATED CHECKPOINT)**

The single most common calendar failure: at W-8, the artist "doesn't have time" for the full arm — so the campaign fires half-loaded and underperforms by 50%. W-8 is the load-sequence gate: if less than 15 of 20 stations are at 7/7 gates, the operation's date slips — not the release date, the *campaign* date. Skip W-8 once and the whole year becomes a fire drill. Never skip it.

**THE QUARTERLY OFFSET (SEASONAL RADIO BEHAVIOR)**

Radio's attention is cyclical: January (post-holiday open), February (spring adds), April (spring campaign), June (summer), September (fall push), October–December (ratings season — cold for unknowns). Operators slot releases into the open windows (Jan/Feb, Apr/May, Sep/Oct) and use the closed windows (Nov/Dec) for proof-building and the annual audit. Releasing into a closed window isn't brave; it's a donation.

**THE CALENDAR AS THE ASCENSION ARTIFACT**

A completed 12-month calendar with 3 armed campaigns, 30+ adds, and a revenue ledger isn't just a personal tool — it's the *ascension evidence* (Module 6.4): the exact artifact that tells a manager, label, or campaign service "this artist runs a professional operation." The unarmed artist gets a mentor. The calendar-running artist gets an investment. Build the artifact.
