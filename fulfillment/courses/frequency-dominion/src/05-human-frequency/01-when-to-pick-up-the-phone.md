# M5L1 — When to pick up the phone

**Module 05 · Human Frequency** · 8 min lesson (1,034 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Call only after a warm email or a local-date hook — 20 seconds, one ask, then shut up.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

The phone is the most misunderstood instrument in radio outreach, and the misunderstanding runs in both directions. Some artists treat every station like a 1997 A&R department that just needs one confident cold call. Others treat the phone like it's radioactive. Both are wrong, and both are expensive. The phone is a precision tool with exactly three legitimate uses, and this lesson is about staying inside them.

The three green lights. Green light one: they replied. An email thread exists, a human engaged, and a call advances it — this is how the worked example's call happens: Marcus replies "send the EPK" on day four, Marisol calls on day five, forty-one seconds, books the conversation that becomes the interview. Green light two: you share the local-date hook — a genuine, timable reason like "I load in at six, I'm ten minutes from the studio, I can bring coffee and a USB stick." Proximity plus a date plus a reason. Green light three: the station publishes an open line — request lines, call-in shows, stations that literally invite calls. If none of the three lights is on, the phone stays down, and the email does the work. That's not timidity. That's trigger discipline.

Now the script itself, and notice it's nearly the same weapon as the pitch — twenty seconds, four moves. Move one, identity in one breath: name, city, genre, and what you sent, so their brain can file you before you finish the sentence. "Marcus — Marisol Vega, the Cincinnati synth artist, EPK landed yesterday for Homegrown Signal." Move two, the ask, singular and bounded: "Ten minutes on the phone before April 17th? I'll take any slot, and I'll keep it to ten." A duration is in the ask — that's the difference between an interview request and an ambush. Move three, the exit, offered sincerely: "If email's better, say so and I'll stop calling." Move four — and this is the one that separates operators from pests — you shut up. Silence after the ask is not awkwardness; it's the sound of a decision being respected. Whoever speaks first after the ask has conceded the frame, and it isn't going to be you.

Twenty seconds. Why so short? Because you are calling someone mid-task at a radio station, where the phone is an interruption by definition. The call's entire job is to convert an email relationship into a calendar entry — not to pitch the song, not to tell the origin story, not to "just connect." Every second past twenty spends goodwill your campaign needs later. If they want to talk longer, they will, and then you follow their lead and enjoy it — hosts are, on the whole, delightful people who got into this because they love talking about music. The twenty-second ceiling is for your side of the street.

Pre-call discipline, three items. One: know your fact sheet cold — runtime, hook timing, date, the ask — because "let me check" on a phone call is a death sentence for a bounded ask. Two: call during business-adjacent hours for staff, or show-adjacent hours for specialty hosts; never morning drive, never live-to-air, never the twenty minutes before an hour turnover when the board is busy. Three: leave one voicemail, maximum, ever — name, the one-line reason, your callback number, and the exit clause, spoken slowly enough to transcribe. Then that door goes back to email, and you never call it again this cycle. One voicemail is persistence. Two is surveillance.

The cringe drill, and I'm going to make you do the thing you'll resist most: record yourself delivering the twenty-second script, play it back, and listen. Yes, you'll hate your voice. Everyone does; voice dislike is the tuition, and the course is worth it, because the playback exposes everything the mirror hides — the run-on identity sentence, the ask that turned into three asks, the "um" before the exit clause, the pace that's too fast because you're nervous. Fix what you hear. Record again. Two rounds and the script becomes yours — not read, owned. Marisol's recorded version runs 23 seconds and she keeps a blooper take where the ask sprawls into a paragraph, because hearing your own sprawl once immunizes you against it on every future call.

What the phone is never for, stated as law because the temptation arrives on bad days. Never a cold call to pitch a song — that's what the email is for, and the email is better at it. Never a call to chase silence — silence is data, not a dial tone shortage, and a "did you get my email" call converts your patient cadence into a panic. Never a call to a personal number — the war room holds professional lines only, per the border rule. Never a call to argue with a no. And never, ever the fantasy version — the "I'll just call every station on the list" afternoon — which is the blast email's meaner cousin, audible spam with your actual voice attached.

Field note on regional culture, because radio is a local business wearing a national costume: community and college stations are often genuinely phone-friendly — half their programming is people calling in — while commercial desks treat unsolicited calls like fire alarms. Your war room's format column already tells you which is which. Specialty hosts are the warmest phones in radio; if you have a green light with a specialty host, use it without fear. They got into this to talk to people who make the music they love. You are not an imposition. You are the good part of their week, if you're bounded about it.

The drill: write your twenty-second script — identity, one bounded ask, exit — record it twice, fix what you hear, and then place one legitimate call this week, green light only. Done means an audio file exists of a sub-25-second take you don't hate, and one call (or one scripted voicemail) is logged in the send log with its result. The phone is now a scalpel in your kit, not a shotgun, and the next lesson is what happens when the call works: the interview, where careers quietly get made.

## Drill — done when

Write the 20-second call script. Record it twice, fix what you hear. Place one green-light call this week.

**Done when:** Done = a sub-25-second recording exists that you'd send a friend, plus one real call or voicemail logged with its result.

### Template

```
“Hi [Name] — [Artist], the [city] [genre] artist, [what you sent] for [show].”
“One ask: [10-minute phoner / confirm the edit landed] before [date]? I’ll keep it to ten.”
“If email’s better, say so and I’ll stop calling.”
→ Then shut up.
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Cold-calling to pitch (“just wanted to make sure you got my email”) — audible spam with your voice attached.

**Fix:** Three green lights only: they replied, a real local-date hook, or a published open line. Otherwise the email does the work. One voicemail, ever.
