# M5L3 — Thank-you economy

**Module 05 · Human Frequency** · 8 min lesson (1,069 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Make champions feel seen — same-day, station-first, zero asks attached — so they stay champions.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Here is a truth that takes artists years to learn and some never do: the add was never the transaction. The relationship is the transaction. A music director who spun you once and felt good about it afterward is worth more than ten who spun you out of obligation, and the difference between those outcomes is almost entirely what you do in the twenty-four hours after they say yes. That's this lesson. The thank-you economy.

The core law: same day, station first, zero asks. Same day because gratitude has a half-life, and a thank-you that arrives Thursday for a Monday favor reads as bookkeeping. Station first because the public framing must always credit the institution before it celebrates you — "Static Bloom spun on The Night Signal, 89.1" before any sentence containing the word "my." And zero asks because a thank-you with a rider isn't a thank-you; it's an invoice wearing a cardigan. The next single, the B-side, the "could you also" — all of it waits. Gratitude unburdened is the rarest email in a director's inbox, and rarity is remembered.

The thank-you email itself is short — five sentences in the example. It names what they did and what it meant, specifically and honestly: "the first Cincinnati add of my life that felt like a door, not a fluke." It tells them what you did with it — the social post, tagged them first, screenshot theirs, with a standing offer to take it down if they'd rather, because some stations have policies and the offer proves you know that. It offers future value with no deadline: a live cut, a fifteen-second ID, a fill-in local for a Friday — concrete things a station actually needs. And it sets the expectation of restraint: "Next single lands in June. It reaches you early, once, no follow-up spam." That last line is the one hosts read twice, because it tells them you're safe to say yes to again.

The social post has its own discipline, and the rules matter because this is where campaigns get sloppy. Station credited first, in the first sentence. Host tagged once — once, not in every reply. Their chart screenshot or their logo, not your album art — the asset belongs to them. Zero asks in the post: no "stream it now," no link-in-bio push on a gratitude post, no vote-begging. And never, under any circumstances, demand a public screenshot from them — "can you post that you played me?" is the fastest way to convert a champion into a cautionary tale. You quote them; you don't summon them. The example post is eleven words plus a tag, and it does more relationship work than a press release: "Cincinnati radio played a Cincinnati song and the drive home felt like a movie. Thank you, Dara."

Then the escalation ladder, for when the relationship warms. The physical note — an actual stamped letter — for an add, a first spin, a meaningful champion moment. In an inbox of forty pitches a day, paper is a nuclear weapon of thoughtfulness; it says someone slowed down. Small and true beats big and generic: name the show, name the date, one line about what their airtime did. And the highest rung, only for genuine ongoing relationships: the drop-by. Coffee for the front desk during a pledge drive. A vinyl copy of the record they broke. Showing up to a station event as a listener, not an artist. These are not tactics — or rather, they only work when they're not tactics. The thank-you economy runs on actually giving a damn, and audiences of one are very good at telling the difference.

Now the ledger discipline that turns gratitude into assets: every thank-you, every social post, every note is logged, because the proof ledger doesn't just hold spins — it holds relationships. The example campaign's day-eleven entry is a thank-you day, and it produces two ledger lines: the WVMR social post, and Marcus's reply to his — "see you on the 31st." Champion maintenance is also maintenance of the machine.

And the edge cases, since real life is edge cases. The silent no-thank-needed case: someone spins you without telling you — you catch it in a chart page or a stream royalty line. Thank them anyway, same day you discover it: "caught the spin on the chart — thank you, it made my month." That thank-you has the highest wow-per-word ratio in radio, because it proves you're paying attention without being asked. The many-thanks case: three stations added you the same week. Each gets their own individual thank-you, station-first, no group emails, no identical paragraphs — the oath applies to gratitude too, and a BCC'd thank-you is somehow worse than a BCC'd pitch. And the no-you-don't-have-to case: a host says "no need to thank me." You thank them anyway, once, briefly. It's not compliance; it's completed communication.

Let me also arm you against the scarcity spiral that ruins this lesson from the inside. Somewhere a voice says: "They spun a lot of people. I'm not special. A thank-you is needy." Flip it with economics. Attention is the scarcest resource in their world, and they spent some of theirs on you. Acknowledging that isn't needy — unacknowledged generosity is the thing that quietly ends relationships, on both sides of the microphone, in every industry humans have built. The thank-you economy has no Inflation problem. Its only failure mode is silence.

One systems note, because champions compound and memory doesn't: your proof ledger needs a relationships page — who championed you, when, what they got, what you sent back. Six months from now, when the second single lands, that page writes your first six emails for you: the champions hear it first, by name, before any cold door does. The thank-you economy's endgame is a small list of stations that feel like colleagues. Every empire in this course is built on that page.

The drill: write your thank-you email template now, today, while nobody has done anything for you yet — five sentences, same-day, station-first, zero asks, restraint-line included. Draft the social post format the same way: station first, tag once, their asset, no asks. Done means both templates live in your swipe file with blanks you could fill in ninety seconds when the first add lands. Because it's coming, and champions are made — or lost — in the twenty-four hours after.

### Template

```
Email: [Name] — thank you. [What they did] meant [specific true thing]. I [what you did: post attached, take-down offered]. If you ever need [live cut / 15s ID / fill-in], I’m [distance] away. Next single lands [month] — early, once, no spam.
Social: station first (sentence 1) · host tagged once · their screenshot · zero asks.
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** The thank-you with a rider — “so glad you loved it! Also could you spin the B-side?” — which converts gratitude into an invoice.

**Fix:** Zero asks for 24 hours, and the only forward-looking line is the restraint promise: next single, early, once, no spam. The ask-free thank-you is what makes the June send welcome.

### Thank-you email + social post

**Email — Dara Whitfield, sent Day 11 (Mar 26) — same day as the add confirmation**
Subject: Thank you — Night Signal, Mar 27

Dara — thank you. ‘Static Bloom’ spinning on The Night Signal is the first Cincinnati add of my life that felt like a door instead of a fluke.

I posted the chart screenshot and tagged the station first (post attached — say the word and it comes down).

If you ever need a live cut, a 15-second ID, or a local for a Friday fill-in, I’m 15 minutes from the studio.

Next single lands in June. It reaches you early, once, with no follow-up spam.

— Marisol

“‘Static Bloom’ spun on The Night Signal — 89.1 WVMR, Friday 10:42pm. Cincinnati radio played a Cincinnati song and the drive home felt like a movie. Thank you, Dara. 📻”
