# M5L2 — Interview that books the next spin

**Module 05 · Human Frequency** · 9 min lesson (1,019 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Give radio a story, not a discography — five answers under 40 seconds, and one pull-quote to take home.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

An interview is not content you graciously provide. It is inventory the host is spending on you, and it either pays them back in tape or it doesn't. That word — tape — is the whole theory. Hosts need usable moments: a clean anecdote they can replay, a local reference their audience can feel, a call-to-action that sounds like a community bulletin, a pull-quote they can drop into imaging between songs for the next month. Give a host tape and you've given them a reason to have you back, which is the actual prize. An interview is not a milestone. It's an audition for a recurring slot.

So preparation is respect. The five answers, each engineered to be speakable in under forty seconds — that constraint isn't arbitrary, it's the length of a radio segment's natural attention unit, and answers that run long force the host to edit you, and edited guests don't get invited back. Answer one: the origin of the song, as one anecdote with one image. Not the nine-month production saga — the moment the song fell out of the sky. Marisol's version: "Driving the Elm Street loop at 1am after a gig nobody came to. The streetlight strobe through a wet windshield — that's the whole song. Nine minutes to write, nine months to finish." That's twenty seconds, it's visual, and any host can riff on it for five minutes without you saying another word. That's tape.

Answer two: the local tie, and be generous with it. The street, the venue, the neighborhood, the DJ who broke you locally — say-on-air ammo. A host programming a Cincinnati audience needs Cincinnati nouns, and you are one of the only guests who can supply them fresh. Answer three: what it sounds like — two references maximum, dial-adjacent, phrased as programming: "Depeche Mode next to Charli XCX next to me, nobody reaches for the dial." You're handing the host a segue into the next song, which is a professional courtesy they feel in their bones even if they never name it. Answer four: the listener call-to-action — one date, one place, one action, no menu. "Foundry on Elm, Friday April 17th. Bring someone you'd drive the loop with." That last clause is a gift-wrapped station liner and hosts know it. Answer five — the one everyone skips and the one that pays rent: the fifteen-second pull-quote, written in advance, delivered clean. "Static Bloom — Friday nights on The Night Signal, 89.1." Song, daypart, station. You just produced their imaging for them.

The closer is a question, and it's the highest-leverage ten seconds of the whole segment: "What do you need from me next month?" Ask it. Mean it. Watch what happens to the host's posture. That question converts you from guest to collaborator — a local artist who thinks in terms of what the show needs — and the answer is always actionable intel: they need live cuts, they need a summer acoustic, they need someone for the pledge drive. Say yes to one thing you can actually deliver, deliver it early, and the recurring door creaks open.

Now the anti-patterns, the interview habits that quietly close doors. The discography tour — "so my first album was really about..." — is a podcast, it's not radio, and nobody's drive-time audience signed up for your catalogue notes. The humility loop — deflecting every compliment with "oh, I'm still learning" — forces the host to carry the segment's energy alone; take the compliment with one sentence of warmth and pivot to something useful. The unbounded answer — two minutes of context before the point — makes the host do surgery on your voice later. And the ask-inflation, smuggling a second ask into the interview ("and if you could also spin the B-side...") — the interview IS the ask being granted; garnish it with requests and you've misunderstood the entire exchange.

Practical logistics, because professional friction kills more interviews than bad answers. Confirm time zone and platform in writing the day before. Test your setup — phone on do-not-disturb, headphones, quiet room, water. Have your fact sheet and the five answers printed or on one screen; never shuffle through notes live. Show up two minutes early. And after: a same-day thank-you that includes, unprompted, the one thing you promised on air plus the offer of the pull-quote clip if their station doesn't clip automatically. The follow-through is the audition after the audition.

One more reframe before the drill. The interview is also — quietly — your best market research session in radio. The host's questions tell you what a professional who loves music found interesting about your story. The off-air small talk tells you what the station needs this quarter. The way they introduce your song tells you how strangers file you. In the example campaign, Marcus's on-air intro — "Cincinnati's own midnight-synth" — hands Marisol her identity sentence, confirmed and third-party-validated, for free. Interviews are listening posts. Bring your ears along with your anecdotes.

A final word on nerves, since the recording of you that you hate and the interview you fear are the same animal. The five-answer format is, underneath everything, a nerves protocol: you are not walking in to improvise, you're walking in to deliver five small prepared gifts and ask one sincere question. Hosts feel the difference instantly — prepared guests relax them, and a relaxed host makes you sound better than you are, which is the quiet magic of good radio. Preparation isn't stiffness. It's the thing that lets your actual personality show up on time.

The drill: write your five answers, timed aloud, each under forty seconds — origin, local tie, sounds-like, call-to-action, pull-quote. Then script the closer question into your notes so it survives nerves: "What do you need from me next month?" Done means a page exists with five timed answers and the closer, and you've read them aloud at least once with a timer. When the interview lands — and by day sixteen of your campaign it may — you'll walk in like someone who's done this before. Because you will have.

### Template

```
1. Origin — one anecdote, one image (≤40s)
2. Local tie — street/venue/DJ nouns the host can say (≤40s)
3. Sounds like — two refs as a segue (≤40s)
4. CTA — one date, one place, one action (≤40s)
5. Pull-quote — song + daypart + station, 15s
Closer: “What do you need from me next month?”
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** The two-minute answer — context upon context until the host has to edit your voice to save the segment.

**Fix:** Five answers engineered at ≤40 seconds, written and timed aloud. The pull-quote is written before the interview, not improvised after it.

## Worked example — Marisol Vega

### Five interview answers, each under 40 seconds

**Where did ‘Static Bloom’ come from?**
“Driving the Elm Street loop at 1am after a gig nobody came to. The streetlight strobe through a wet windshield — that’s the whole song. It took nine minutes to write and nine months to finish.”

**What does Cincinnati mean to this record?**
“Everything local is load-bearing. Verse one names the loop. The record was mixed on Elm. If a host needs one line on air: ‘it’s a Cincinnati song that sounds like Cincinnati at 1am.’”

**What does it sound like?**
“Midnight-synth — dark 80s pop with a modern low end. If you played Depeche Mode next to Charli XCX next to me, nobody reaches for the dial.”

**What should listeners do?**
“Foundry on Elm, Friday April 17. Bring someone you’d drive the loop with. If they can’t come, the song is everywhere April 18.”

**Pull-quote for imaging**
““Static Bloom — Friday nights on The Night Signal, 89.1.” Fifteen seconds, cold ending, sweeps clean.”
