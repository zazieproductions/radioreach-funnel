# M5L4 — Repairing a burned well

**Module 05 · Human Frequency** · 8 min lesson (1,088 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace) · **Before Day 1, if needed**

**Outcome:** If you already spammed, write the apology that can reopen a door — then actually go silent.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

This is the lesson I hope you never need, and the one that redeems this entire course for the artists who do. If you have already burned a market — blasted two hundred contacts with the same email, chased silence with "just bumping this," argued with a no, or bought a "radio campaign" from somebody who did it for you — you are not disqualified. You are carrying damage, and damage is repairable. Not instantly, and not with a coupon. With one email, one apology, and ninety days of demonstrated silence.

First, the honest diagnosis, because repair begins with naming what you actually did. Spam, to a station, is not defined by your intent; it's defined by their experience: email that is not for them, not about their show, and not stoppable. If you sent identical paragraphs to forty humans, you told forty humans they were interchangeable — whatever your heart was doing at the time. If you followed up four times in a week, you trained them that a yes was the only exit. If you're mortgaged to a pay-for-play service that blasts on your behalf, hear this clearly: purchased-play campaigns are spam with a receipt, and the receipt is in your name. Diagnosis hurts. Do it anyway, on paper, because vague guilt produces vague apologies, and vague apologies repair nothing.

Now the apology itself, and its architecture is unforgiving, so I'll be precise. Four moves. Move one: own it in one line, concretely — the specific act, the actual count, no passive voice. "In October I BCC'd you and forty other stations the same 'please consider my single' email. Twice." Not "if I ever spammed you" — nobody accepts an apology for a hypothetical. Move two: name the harm from their side of the desk. "That was disrespectful to you and to the late-set audience you program for." The apology must prove you understand what it cost them — their time, their trust, the audience's time. Move three: one relevant track, honestly framed, with permission to ignore attached. "If one relevant track is ever useful: link — a 3:24 midnight-synth record, honestly an edge-fit for your format." Note the honesty: the example doesn't pretend the burned station is a perfect format match, because burned contacts smell pretense at parts per billion. And move four: the silence pledge, with a number in it. "Ignore it with my blessing. You will not hear from me for ninety days."

Then — and this is the actual repair — you go silent for ninety days. The email is ten percent of the operation. Ninety days of demonstrated silence is the other ninety, because you're not asking them to believe your words; you're asking them to believe your calendar. Every day of the pledge that passes is evidence. And when the ninety days end, you do not get a victory lap; you get one carefully targeted, correctly formatted, oath-compliant send on your next release, and even that is a maybe. Some wells stay closed forever. That's the price, and paying it in full — without complaint, without a fourth move — is what makes the next market's doors safer.

The timing question, answered with discipline: repairs go out before your campaign, not during. The example places the apology in the pre-campaign period — it's a Day-minus operation, not a Day-8 improvisation. If the burn happened years ago and you've since learned better, one clean apology still works, because stations keep longer memories than artists do. If the burn is fresh — you blasted them last month — wait. A repair email forty-eight hours after the offense reads as a mood swing; one at ninety days, with the new work ready, reads as growth. And if you're mid-campaign right now, mid-spam, hearing this for the first time: stop sending today. Full stop. The best repair moment is always now-plus-discipline; the second best is now.

What repair never includes, so you don't smuggle it in out of habit: no excuses with weather in them ("we were between managers"), no blame-shifting to a service you paid ("the campaign company sent those" — you hired them; own it), no attachment, no link to a streaming profile with the song buried three deep, no "I know you're busy" padding, no second apology when the first goes unanswered — the unanswered apology is not a wound; it's the silence you pledged, starting early.

And the systemic point, because this course has kept one oath from hour one: you're not just repairing your access. Every indie who burns a market makes the next three artists' pitches heavier. Directors who get burned stop reading strangers. Your repair, honored in full, is a tiny tax rebate for the whole ecosystem you're about to operate in — and conversely, the no-spam oath you took in Module 01 exists precisely so you never have to come back to this lesson except as a tourist. New artists watching this fresh: this lesson is your smallpox vaccination. Learn the shape of the apology so you recognize the shape of the disease.

And a boundary case worth naming: the apology you're tempted to send to someone who was never actually wronged — who simply never replied. Silence is not damage. Do not apologize for it, do not "clear the air" with people whose air was never dirty; that email is a fourth touch wearing a hairshirt. The burned-well repair is for real burns — identifiable acts, with counts, that you'd wince to read aloud. If you can't name the act, there's no well to repair, just a mirror to look at. Most "I think I burned that bridge" feelings dissolve under the honest question: what exactly did I send, to whom, how many times? Write the answer down before you write any apology.

The drill is conditional, deliberately. If you have a burned contact — and you know, in your chest, whether you do — write the apology today: four moves, forty to eighty words, the ninety-day number in it. Send it only if every word is true, then write the pledge-end date on your calendar and honor it like a contract, because it is one. If you have no burned wells, write nothing and send nobody — your drill is to re-read the oath and note the date of your last verified-clean send in the log. Either way, one artifact: the apology in your swipe file, or the clean-record note. The floor of this course is honesty. This lesson is where it gets load-bearing.

## Drill — done when

If you have a burned contact: write the 4-move apology (40–80 words, 90-day pledge) and send it only if true. If not: log your clean-record note.

**Done when:** Done = either the apology exists in your swipe file with a calendar pledge-end date, or your send log carries a dated clean-record note.

### Template

```
“[Name] — in [month] I [the specific act, with the count]. That was disrespectful to you and to [their audience].
I won’t do it again. If one relevant track is ever useful: [link] — a [runtime] [genre] record, honestly [format-fit honesty].
Ignore it with my blessing. You will not hear from me for 90 days.”
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** The apology with an asterisk — blame shifted to a campaign company, or a follow-up apology when the first goes unanswered.

**Fix:** Four moves, no excuses, no second send. The unasked follow-up IS the spam you're apologizing for. The 90-day silence is the repair; the email is only its announcement.

**To:** Louise Achtenberg (WJHR) — stand-in for any burned contact
Subject: I blasted you last year — owning it, one track, then silence

Louise — in October I BCC’d you and forty other stations the same ‘please consider my single’ email. Twice. That was disrespectful to you and to the late-set audience you program for.

I won’t do it again. If one relevant track is ever useful: vega.link/staticbloom — a 3:24 midnight-synth record, honestly an edge-fit for your format.

Ignore it with my blessing. You will not hear from me for 90 days.

— Marisol Vega
