# M6L2 — Airplay to income

**Module 06 · Empire Loop** · 9 min lesson (1,011 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace) · **Post-campaign**

**Outcome:** Route radio credibility into gigs, sync, and clients — the badge, cited properly, at a rate.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Let's retire a fantasy before we build the reality. Radio does not pay independent artists. The royalties from a handful of specialty spins buy dinner, generously interpreted. If you came here for radio-as-income, the arithmetic was never going to rescue you. But radio as a badge — a third-party, verifiable credential that strangers in your region already respect — that asset converts into real money through exactly three doors: venues, sync, and clients. This lesson is how you carry the badge through each one without fumbling it.

Why the badge works: trust transfer. A booker doesn't know you. A sync supervisor doesn't know you. A small-business owner definitely doesn't know you. But they all know what "spinning on 89.1" means: a third-party professional in music listened and said yes, in public, on a signal with call letters. Radio is pre-verified taste. Your job is never to oversell the badge — one line, one citation, specific station and show — because the whole power of the credential is that it sounds like evidence, not advertising. The moment it sounds like advertising, you've spent it badly.

Door one: venues. The venue email in your swipe file is the model, and its anatomy is worth dissecting. It leads with the draw logic — "why it fills a room" — and the badge appears as one load-bearing fact among several: the spin, the prior add, the regional listener percentage, the honest draw estimate. Note what the email does not do: it doesn't claim a number it can't back, doesn't attach a press kit, doesn't open with the radio line like a trumpet. "Spinning on 89.1 WVMR's The Night Signal, charted three weeks, 61% of my listeners in Ohio, honest draw 40 to 60 on a Saturday" — a booker reads that paragraph and sees a professional who understands their actual problem, which is Friday night's headcount, not your art. The badge gets you read. The draw logic gets you booked. And when you're negotiating — guarantee versus door split, the room size for the next run — the ledger is your rate card: "last run sold out a 240-cap with three weeks of radio behind it" is a sentence that moves offers.

Door two: sync and licensing. Supervisors need regional truth and clean files. Your radio facts translate almost literally: "61% of streaming listeners in Ohio" is media-buyer language for "this artist moves units in this region," and a radio-clean 3:24 with a pre-built 30-second hook is a deliverable they don't have to produce. The client-email swipe shows the shape: numbers first, "not vibes," one bounded ask — fifteen minutes to play the hook against the brief. Small supervisors, regional brands, dealerships, local ad agencies: this is a volume game of modest, real checks, and the badge makes you legible to people who otherwise would never open an artist's email. You are not selling your music to them. You are selling verified regional traction with broadcast-ready files attached.

Door three: clients and freelance work — the least glamorous door and, month to month, often the one that actually pays the rent. If you do any music-adjacent work for money — production, custom pieces, audio branding, teaching, session work — radio proof repositions you from "freelancer who needs work" to "working artist with credentials." The same numbers-first email applies: what you did, where it aired, what you can build for them. There is no shame in this door. There is only sequencing: the badge gets you the meeting, the meeting gets you the rate, and the rate buys the studio time that makes the next record that earns the next badge. That's the loop, funding itself.

Now the discipline that keeps all three doors open: cite the station precisely and honestly, every time. "Spinning on 89.1 WVMR's The Night Signal" — real station in the example's fictional universe, real show, real duration. Never "charting on radio stations nationwide" when the truth is one specialty add in one city, because the exaggerated badge is worse than none: it reads as inflatable, and inflatable is disqualifying in every one of these markets. One true citation outperforms three fogged ones. And never imply endorsement — the station played your song; they didn't ask you to rep them. When in doubt, the phrasing that never gets old: "recent specialty adds include [station], [station], and [station]." Let the list grow instead of the adjectives.

Rhythm matters as much as approach, so here's the post-campaign timing. Week one after the campaign closes: the venue email goes out while the spin is still charting and the date logic is live — "three weeks of radio behind an April 17th show" has a shelf life, and the badge is freshest now. Weeks two through four: sync and client emails, which are calendar-agnostic and can run on a monthly tick forever. And continuously: the ledger gets every result, because a venue reply that says "come back in the fall" is a warm door with a date, which is this module's native currency.

The long game deserves a name: local legend status. The compounding endpoint of this system isn't a national tour — it's being the artist in your region whose name radio people say comfortably, bookers trust instinctively, and supervisors call when they need something real. That status is buildable in one market in roughly a year of correct cycles, and it pays for a decade. Ask any scene's veteran booker about the artist who "always had their stuff together" — that's who you're becoming, and this door-by-door routing is the mechanism.

The drill: write one venue email and one client email citing a specific station — use the two swipes as scaffolding, fill them with your true facts, and send the venue one this week while your campaign's iron is hot. Done means two emails exist in your outbox-ready state with one precise station citation each, honest numbers, and a single bounded ask. The Vault opened the door. The campaign earned the badge. This lesson walks you through carrying it — with a rate.

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Inflating the badge — “charting nationwide” off one specialty add — which reads as inflatable and disqualifies you in all three markets.

**Fix:** One precise citation per email: station, show, duration. “Recent specialty adds include [station], [station]” — let the list grow, never the adjectives.
