# M6L3 — Release calendar as a radio calendar

**Module 06 · Empire Loop** · 8 min lesson (1,056 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Never drop a single again without its T-28 radio skeleton attached — lead time is the strategy.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

From now on, in your career, there is no such thing as a release date. There is only a release season, and the radio calendar is its skeleton. Because here's the error this lesson exists to kill: the Friday-morning artist, song out everywhere, now wondering "should I email some stations?" — three weeks after the only window in which radio could have helped. Radio wants lead time. Specialty shows plan a week or two out, college adds run on semester rhythms, and your pitch's strongest card — "why this week" — is strongest before the song is already last week's news. If you cannot staff the lead time, delay the drop. That sentence is the whole lesson, and the rest of it is the schedule that makes the sentence survivable.

The skeleton, T-minus style, anchored to release day. T-42: pick the single and lock the radio edit — the clock-fit cut, the clean pass, the cold ending — because everything downstream needs frozen assets. T-28: clone the war room — this campaign's twenty, refreshed by the ledger's warm doors and the Vault's re-verification; four weeks out is when contacts get checked, addresses confirmed, scores updated. T-21: first sends — the full Module 04 cadence starts here, three weeks of airtime runway before the song is public. T-14: Touch 2, plus the EPK refresh — the ledger's new rows rotate into the proof block, so the pitch that goes out now is measurably stronger than last cycle's. T-7: Touch 3, new information only, and by now, if the campaign is working, you have chart positions to cite — the song is "already spinning" before it's officially out, which is the single most powerful state an independent release can occupy. T-0: release day — thank-yous staged, war-room statuses frozen for the audit, your socials riding the radio you've already earned instead of begging for it cold. T+2: the thank-you wave, the ledger update, every screenshot filed.

Read the skeleton again and notice what it actually says: your release day is a Thursday-night museum opening where the paintings have already been reviewed. The radio campaign is not a promo afterthought bolted onto a release — it is the pre-load that makes release day land with impact already banked. Artists who run this calendar stop experiencing release weeks as coin flips, because the coin was flipped three weeks ago, professionally, in writing, to twenty named humans.

Now the honest staffing question, because the skeleton has a cost and pretending it doesn't is how artists abandon calendars mid-year. The full T-28-to-T-plus-2 cycle costs roughly the same ten operator hours as campaign one — four forty-five-minute blocks a week for three weeks — plus a fourth week of light follow-through. If your life cannot staff ten hours across a month, the correct move is not to shrink the skeleton; it's to shrink the war room: fifteen doors, or ten, run at full quality. A ten-station campaign executed completely beats a thirty-station campaign abandoned on day nine, and the skeleton flexes down honestly. What it doesn't do is flex into "I'll do radio next week sometime," because sometime is where campaigns go to not exist.

Two structural decisions to make now, on purpose, so the calendar runs you instead of vice versa. First: release cadence. The rhythm that feeds radio best is a single every eight to twelve weeks — enough spacing that each song gets its own twenty-one days, enough frequency that hosts learn your name across a year. Two singles a year cannot compound; twelve burn out your war room and your songs. Pick a number between four and seven for year one and put the actual dates on the actual calendar today — the example maps June's single onto the skeleton with dates, and it took eleven minutes. Second: the stagger rule. Never launch a radio campaign the same week as a tour or a major video; each lever deserves its own three-week window, and stacking them splits your attention exactly when the cadence needs its forty-five minutes. The calendar is a sequencer. Sequence it.

The audit connection, briefly, because lesson four completes it: every campaign's numbers feed the next campaign's war room. Reply-lag data tells you when touches should land. Format data tells you which doors deserve the A-game. The skeleton is not just repeated — it's tuned, cycle over cycle, and by campaign four you will know your market's rhythms better than most labels' regional departments, which is not an exaggeration, it's just what twenty-one days of logged attention per quarter accumulates into.

And the discipline tool that makes the whole calendar real: the T-minus release map, one page, printed next to the war room — T-42, T-28, T-21, T-14, T-7, T-0, T+2, each with its date and its one-line action. The example's map is in the drawer: single two, June, seven rows, dates filled. When the map is on the wall, "release season" stops being a mood and becomes a checklist with dates on it — and checklists with dates are the only plans that survive contact with an artist's actual life.

One calendar honesty note, because someone watching has a release already scheduled and is doing math with dread: if your drop is inside T-14 right now, you do not get the full skeleton — and you don't need it. Run the compressed version: a ten-door war room of only your warmest doors (champions plus the best scores from your ledger), first sends the day you finish the edit, hook-cut Touch 2 at day six, one new-info Touch 3 at day ten, and the T+2 thank-you wave no matter what. Smaller machine, same oath, same cadence shape — and next release gets the full skeleton, because the deadline you're missing this time is the deadline you'll never miss again.

The drill: map your next release onto the T-minus skeleton — real dates, seven rows, one action each — and put the four work-block weeks on your calendar before you close the lesson. If you have no next release scheduled, the drill is to pick the date now; the calendar creates the deadline, the deadline creates the campaign, and the campaign — as you now know — creates the career. Done means the printed map exists with dates. Amateur calendars create amateur careers. Yours just became architecture.

## Drill — done when

Map your next release onto the T-28 skeleton with real dates. Put the four work-block weeks on the calendar.

**Done when:** Done = a printed T-minus map exists with seven dated rows and the work-block weeks are on your actual calendar. Photo it.

### Template

```
T-42 pick single + lock radio edit · T-28 clone war room (verify contacts) · T-21 first sends · T-14 Touch 2 + EPK refresh · T-7 Touch 3 (cite chart facts) · T-0 release + thank-yous staged · T+2 thank-you wave + ledger.
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Song drops Friday; “now let’s email some stations” — three weeks after the window where radio could compound the release.

**Fix:** The skeleton is non-negotiable; the war room is flexible. No time to staff 20 doors? Run 10, completely. No time to run any? Delay the drop — the calendar is the strategy.
