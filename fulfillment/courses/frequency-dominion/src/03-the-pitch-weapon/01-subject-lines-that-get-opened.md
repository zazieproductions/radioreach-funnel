# M3L1 — Subject lines that get opened

**Module 03 · The Pitch Weapon** · 9 min lesson (1,042 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Write 11 subject lines from the local-artist swipe logic — then keep 3 and never reuse a loser.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Your campaign lives or dies in one point four seconds. That's the thumb-decision window on a music director's phone, and it is spent entirely on your subject line. Not your song. Not your bio. Not the ninety words you bled over. Nine words, more or less, between queued and deleted. So we start the weapons module where the war is actually fought.

First, the morgue. Walk it with me, because you've sent at least one of these. "New single for your consideration" — consideration is their word to give, not yours to request. "Demo attached" — nothing was attached, and demos are for labels. "Checking in" — you are not their pharmacy. "Unsigned artist seeking airplay" — you just filed yourself under problem. "🔥🔥🔥 NEW HEAT 🔥🔥🔥" — no. Every one of these subject lines commits the same crime: it's about you, addressed to nobody, proving nothing. The delete thumb isn't cruel. It's efficient. Your subject line failed to give it a reason to pause.

Now the anatomy of a line that lives. Four moves, and you need at least two per line. Move one: name the show. "For The Night Signal" proves you know what you're asking to be on — you're not blasting, you're submitting to a specific program with a specific audience. Move two: prove the clock. A runtime — 3:24 — in the subject line is a credential. It says "I know what your blocks are and I built for them." No other artist's subject line carries a runtime. Move three: prove the ground. City, venue, date — localism in nine words. Move four: name the human. "Dara — one track, one ask" reads like a colleague, because it is one.

Specificity is respect. That's the entire theory. Every concrete fact in a subject line is proof you did homework, and homework is the only etiquette a director with forty unread emails recognizes instantly.

So the exercise: eleven lines, filled from your identity sentence and your war room. The workbook gives you the eleven templates. Write all eleven — not three good ones, eleven, because volume is how the good ones surface. Then the cull: kill eight, keep three. One for your A-list first sends, one built for Touch 2, one carrying new information for Touch 3. The cull matters because a subject line you love and don't send becomes your control group: when your kept lines outperform the pattern you almost used, you've learned something about your market you'll use for years.

Here are the example campaign's eleven, so you can hear the register: "Cincinnati midnight-synth for The Night Signal — 3:24." Show, genre, city, clock — four moves, one line, that's the A-lister. "Interview window Apr 14–16 + 'Static Bloom'" — a bounded ask with a deadline, which hosts respect disproportionately. "30-second hook for Friday night — Marisol Vega" — the Touch 2 line, built for the laziest possible yes. "New info: Queen City Beat quote + bigger room Apr 17" — Touch 3, new information only, per the oath. Notice what is absent: no "please," no "opportunity," no emojis, no ALL CAPS desperation, and no false urgency. Radio people get urgency from the news. From artists, they get schedules.

Now the deployment discipline, because eleven lines deployed badly is still spam. The kept three go to your A-list only — the fours and fives from your scoring. Across the campaign you'll watch which line shape opens with your market: runtime-forward, date-forward, or name-forward. That's market intelligence, and it's free. The rule that keeps it clean: never reuse a loser on the same contact. If Dara didn't open "Cincinnati midnight-synth for The Night Signal," the Touch 2 to Dara is not that line again with "RE:" bolted on. It's a different move — the hook line, the name line, or the new-info line. A repeated loser tells the thumb you're a loop, and thumbs are ruthless with loops.

Two technical notes and then the drill. First, length: aim under about forty characters visible on mobile — roughly eight words. Your brilliance past word nine is invisible on the device where the decision happens. Second, never put a link in a subject line, never use reply-to-a-thread-you-don't-belong-in tricks, and never write "RE:" on a fresh email. Those tricks work exactly once, on exactly one person, and the person is never the director — it's the spam filter, and it works on you.

One more calibration, because subject lines are where artists oscillate between boring and cringe. Boring-but-specific beats clever-but-fogged, every time, in this market. "Local date Apr 17 (Foundry on Elm) — radio edit 3:24" is not going to win a copywriting award. It is going to get opened by a host who books local music, because it is a completely legible offer: here is a local artist, here is a real venue, here is a radio-ready file. The subject line's job is not to be adored. It's to be filed correctly in one point four seconds: not spam, local, fits the clock, one ask.

A quick word on A/B honesty, since I promised you market intelligence and intelligence needs clean data. An A/B test on twenty sends is directional, not statistical — if line A opens three of ten and line B opens one of ten, that's a lean, not a law. So treat the kept three as a rotation you watch across the whole campaign and beyond: runtime-forward tends to win with rotation-minded MDs, date-forward with community programmers, name-forward with specialty hosts who answer humans. By campaign three you'll know your market's taste in subject lines better than any consultant could tell you — and unlike the consultant, you'll have the send log to prove it.

The drill: write your eleven in the swipe file workbook. Kill eight, keep three, and assign each keeper to first-send, Touch 2, or Touch 3. Then the done-check is brutal and simple: paste your three keepers at the top of your war room, where they stare at you every time you sit down to send. If one makes you wince when you look at it next Tuesday, that's the cull working — swap in the best of your dead eight and carry on. Eleven written, three kept, zero reused losers.

### Template

```
1. [City] [genre] for [show] — [runtime]
2. Interview window [dates] + “[song]”
3. Local date [venue] [date] — radio edit [runtime]
4. Follow-up: still a fit for [show]?
5. 30-second hook for [daypart] — [Artist]
6. [Station] specialty: “[song]” ([other station] add last [period])
7. Load-in [city] [date] — live cut + radio edit
8. Replacement link — “[song]” [runtime]
9. [First name] — one track, one ask
10. Hometown [genre] for this week’s [show]
11. New info: [quote/chart/room] + “[song]”
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Cleverness creep — the line wins a copywriting award and tells the thumb nothing about show, city, or clock.

**Fix:** Eight words, at least two of the four moves. If it could sit atop any artist's email to any station, it fails; add the show, the runtime, or the date.
