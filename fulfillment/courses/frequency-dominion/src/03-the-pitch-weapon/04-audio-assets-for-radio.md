# M3L4 — Audio assets for radio

**Module 03 · The Pitch Weapon** · 8 min lesson (1,077 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Deliver broadcast-ready files with metadata so clean a host can find you in the dark.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Let's talk about the fifteen minutes after a host decides to like your song — the fifteen minutes where most independent artists lose the add. The host thinks: fine, I'll play it. They go to your link. And now, one by one, the amateur landmines detonate. The file is a streaming link that can't be downloaded. The MP3 is a 128 with the intro clipped. The filename is final_FINAL_v3 REAL ONE.mp3. The metadata says Track 04. There's no hook cut, so building a promo would mean the host editing audio themselves — which they will not do. And the decision, which was yes, quietly becomes "maybe someday," and someday never comes on a deadline.

Radio-ready is not a vibe. It's a deliverables list, and it's short. One: the WAV — 44.1 kilohertz, 24-bit, true peak around minus one dB, because broadcast chains have their own processing and headroom is politeness. Two: the MP3 — 320 kilobits, because half the humans at community and college stations will grab the MP3 and go; make the lazy path the good path. Both carry the full radio edit — your clock-fit cut from Module 01, clean lyrics pass done, cold ending, no forty-second fade into conceptual silence. Three: the 30-second hook — starts at 0:32 or wherever the chorus erupts, ends cold, exists for imaging and for Touch 2. You cut it once; every host who ever wants a stinger thanks you. Four: the one-sheet PDF from the last lesson. That's the arsenal. Four files, one folder, one link.

Metadata is where respect lives, and it's free. Every file carries: artist, title, runtime in the title if you like, ISRC if you have one, and one contact email that will still work in six months. Why is metadata load-bearing? Because radio libraries, automation systems, and yes, piracy-adjacent file-sharing between hosts all strip context. A WAV named properly with embedded artist and title survives being dropped into someone's overnight system; Track_04.wav becomes a mystery that gets deleted during a library purge at 2am. You are not just delivering audio. You are delivering the answer to "what is this and who do I thank" for every system and human that touches it.

Naming convention, and this is law: Artist_Song_Version_Runtime. VegaMarisol_StaticBloom_RadioEdit_3-24.wav. Ugly? Slightly. Functional? Completely — it sorts, it identifies, it survives a download folder of forty submissions from a music department's Tuesday. No spaces, no apostrophes, no emoji, no "master" unless it is, and absolutely never finalFINAL anything. The filename is the suit your song wears to the interview. Iron the suit.

Now the QC pass, which takes eleven minutes and saves campaigns. Listen to the actual export, not the session — the whole file, headphones once, phone speaker once, car if you have one. Check the first second and the last second specifically: intros that start mid-word and outros with a clipped fade are the classic export bugs. Verify loudness feels competitive but not crushed — if your master is smashing a brick wall, the broadcast processor will make it worse; dynamics are your friend over FM. Confirm the metadata shows correctly in a player — not in your DAW's export dialog, in an actual player. Then, and only then, upload to the one link.

One more file discipline point: version control that a stranger can read. When Touch 3 offers "a different edit" or a host asks for the clean version after all, you are not exporting a new mystery — you're adding VegaMarisol_StaticBloom_CleanEdit_3-24.wav to the same folder with its own correct metadata. The folder is the product. Its cleanliness is part of your pitch whether you know it or not, because hosts talk: "sent me a folder, everything was labeled, hook cut included, easy" is a sentence that gets you forwarded.

What about chart reporting and stations that want files through a distributor or a submission platform? Two answers. If the station's policy says submit through their form — you submit through their form, oath rules, and your folder link rides in the form's URL field. If a station wants a specific delivery system, the Vault notes say so, and you comply exactly, because format compliance is the cheapest credibility available. Your assets are format-agnostic; your delivery bends to their door.

The example campaign's audio folder is in the worked example drawer: five entries, the three cuts plus the one-sheet, one link, every file named to convention, metadata embedded, and — the detail I want you to steal — the whole folder tested by sending it to one friend with instructions to "play this on your phone like you're busy." If the busy test passes — song identifies itself, hook arrives, nothing breaks — you are radio-ready in a way 95% of submissions never are.

And the final word on the hook cut, since it's the asset artists skip and the one hosts love most. The 30-second version is not a truncated song; it's a manufactured tease. Start where the chorus erupts — 0:32 in the example — end cold on the hook's last hit, and let the silence do the selling. Its jobs: Touch 2's entire payload ("30-second hook if that's easier"), imaging ammo for hosts who build promos, and the three-second audition when a director opens your folder on a smoke break. A great hook cut has closed adds the full song never got the chance to. Ninety minutes of edit work, three jobs, forever. Cut it today.

One habit worth stealing from station engineers: the checksum ritual, artist edition. Before any file leaves your machine, play the first second, the last second, and one spot-check in the middle of the exact file you're about to upload — not the session, the file. Export bugs live at the edges: the clipped first word, the fade that didn't render, the version that was actually the wrong take. Eleven minutes of edge-checking across four files, and you never once have to send the career-limiting email that begins "so sorry, wrong bounce." Every operator has that email in them. The good ones never have to send it.

The drill: export the radio edit and the 30-second hook today, named to convention, metadata embedded, uploaded to your one link. Run the busy test on one friend. Done means the link opens on a phone to a folder where a host could go from curious to broadcast without replying to you first. That folder is the last weapon. Module 04 fires them all.

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Sending a streaming link that can’t be downloaded, or an unlabeled bounce named finalFINAL3.mp3.

**Fix:** Four files, one folder, one link, named Artist_Song_Version_Runtime, metadata embedded. If a host can’t reach a broadcast-ready file in two taps, the yes dies in the fifteen minutes after they liked you.
