# M4L2 — Days 1–7: first blood sends

**Module 04 · The 21-Day Campaign** · 9 min lesson (1,014 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace) · **Campaign days 1–7**

**Outcome:** Send 20 A-list emails on schedule — 10 on Day 1, 10 on Day 3 — and log every one.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Day one. Ten emails. Not nine, not a hopeful fourteen — ten, to your ten highest-scoring doors, and the reason for the number is the reason for the whole module: batch discipline produces clean data and sane operators. Ten on Day 1, ten on Day 3, and the twenty-one days have begun.

The send block itself, minute by minute, because I want you to see how small this is. Minutes zero to ten: open the war room, confirm your first ten rows, and read each contact's notes column again — the Vault's micro-facts, your why-this-station fragments. Minutes ten to forty: ten sends, one at a time, each with its fresh personal first line — the listening sentence from your pitch's block two — written per human, about their actual show. The frozen body carries the song, the proof, the link, the ask. Minutes forty to forty-five: the log. Station, name, subject used, touch one, time sent. Five minutes, ten rows, done.

Now the rules that keep first blood clean. Rule one: one ask per email, and it's the frozen one — spin, interview, or feedback, chosen per format back when you wrote the pitch. No new asks invented mid-send. Rule two: no attachments, ever — the one link is the delivery. Rule three: send from your real address, plain text or very close to it, and hit send yourself; the oath's no-automation clause applies to volume and to tools. Rule four: personal line means personal — the segue she actually heard, the local hour he actually hosts. If you find yourself copy-pasting the "personal" line between contacts, stop, walk, come back; that's the spam reflex wearing your handwriting.

Expectation setting, and this is the real lesson of week one: silence is data. Statistically, most of your twenty will not reply this week, and a plurality will never reply at all — not because your song failed, but because inboxes are weather systems and you are one interesting cloud. The campaign was designed for this: the three-touch cadence exists because silence on day two is not a verdict, it's a Tuesday. In the worked example, Marisol's first week produces exactly one human reply — Marcus Boone, day four, "send the EPK and the WAV, no promises" — out of twenty sends. Five percent reply rate in week one, and the campaign ends with an add, an interview, and an imaging ask. That's not despite the slow start. That's the normal shape of a working campaign: quiet week one, live threads in weeks two and three.

Day two, and I need to hold your hand away from the button: do not follow up. Day two is the log-verification day — links tested in a private window, statuses moved from queued to sent, maybe two personal lines drafted for batch two. The urge to "just check they got it" forty hours later is the anxiety loop that hardens into pestering by Friday. The cadence has room for exactly three touches across twenty-one days. Touch two belongs to day eight or later, and nothing you feel on day two changes that math.

Day three: the second ten, to the rows scored two and three. Same frozen pitch, tighter personalization budget — the template-tight send from Module 02 is correct and honorable here; the war room already told you where the bespoke effort pays. Same log discipline. And now the whole war room reads sent, twenty for twenty, and the campaign's actual work — the waiting, the logging, the touching — becomes a rhythm instead of an event.

Days four through seven are reply handling and quiet. A reply gets an answer within hours, not days: thank, deliver anything asked — EPK, WAV, hook — and advance exactly one step. Marcus asks for the EPK; Marcus gets the EPK same day, one line, no second ask riding along. A no gets the one-line thank-you and a status change, door open, next cycle. And silence gets what silence deserves from an operator: a log entry, a status glance, and a life lived. You have music to make. The campaign is a machine; your job this week was done in forty-five-minute blocks.

The weekly audit on day seven — Sunday, fifteen minutes, photo ritual. Count the honest numbers: sends, replies, opens-if-evident, silence. Write two sentences in the log's notes field: what the numbers say, and what you refuse to conclude yet. Week one is too early to judge ammunition; it exists to populate the board. Marisol's day seven reads: twenty sent, two human replies, eighteen pending, zero burned, on pace. That sentence takes ninety seconds to write and it is the difference between a campaign and a mood.

One more thing, the human thing. Somewhere in days five or six a specific silence will start to itch — the one station you wanted, the one host whose taste you admire, nothing back. Write that station's name on a sticky note, put it under the war room's cut list, and leave it there until day fourteen. The itch is information about your attachments, not their verdict. Touch 3 is where one new-information email goes to the door you love, and between now and then, the machine carries it so you don't have to.

A practical note on send timing, since you'll ask: Tuesday through Thursday mornings, local station time, is the classic high-open window — Monday is a triage day nobody survives, Friday afternoon is a ghost town, and weekends are for specialty hosts who live differently, which your war room knows row by row. Marisol sends Monday 6pm anyway in the example, because a campaign that runs on your real calendar beats a perfect one that runs on nobody's — but if your blocks are movable, move them into the window. Small edge, free edge, worth taking.

The drill, day one: send ten, log ten. The done-definition is a send log with ten rows timestamped today and a war room photo showing ten statuses moved. Then day three, ten more. First blood isn't dramatic. It's administrative. That's exactly why it works.

## Drill — done when

Day 1: send 10 A-list emails, personal first lines, log all 10. Day 3: send the next 10. Day 7: audit.

**Done when:** Done = send log holds 20 timestamped Touch-1 rows and a Day-7 audit note exists. Screenshot the log.

### Template

```
Send block (45 min): 0–10 review rows + notes → 10–40 ten sends w/ fresh personal lines → 40–45 log 10 rows.
Log row: date · station · contact · subject · touch · time · status
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Day-2 follow-up itch — “just confirming you got it” 40 hours later, which reads as pestering and spends Touch 2 early.

**Fix:** The cadence owns the calendar: Touch 2 waits for Day 8–10. Day 2 is for log verification and drafting batch 2's personal lines only.
