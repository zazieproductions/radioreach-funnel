# M4L1 — Week 0: load the war room

**Module 04 · The 21-Day Campaign** · 8 min lesson (1,004 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace) · **Campaign Week 0**

**Outcome:** Load, freeze, and calendar everything — so Day 1 is execution, not decisions.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Nothing gets sent this week. I want that on the wall in letters: Week Zero is for loading. Because the difference between a campaign and a burst of enthusiasm is that a campaign has already decided everything before its first email goes out. Enthusiasm decides things at 11pm on Day 3, badly, while tired. This week we move every decision you can possibly make in advance into the bank, so that Days 1 through 21 are pure execution — the least glamorous and most lethal structure in this entire course.

Five loads. Load one: the Vault filter, run. Hometown metro, ninety-minute ring, weekend ladder — the heat map from Module 02, finalized, with the twenty survivors sitting in your war room, scored, cut list written on, status column reading queued across all twenty rows. If any row can't honestly say queued — no named human, unverified address, draft not ready — the row isn't ready, and this week exists precisely to fix that, row by row.

Load two: assets frozen. The radio edit, exported and QC'd — Module 01's clock fit, Module 03's metadata discipline. The 30-second hook, cut. The one-sheet, printed to PDF, civilian-tested. The pitch, version one, under ninety words, read aloud, frozen — you do not touch it mid-campaign. The three subject-line keepers, assigned to first send, Touch 2, Touch 3. When I say frozen I mean it strategically: identical ammunition across all twenty doors is what makes your Day 18 audit mean anything. Change the weapon mid-war and you'll never know which weapon worked.

Load three: the calendar, blocked. Four forty-five-minute work blocks per week for three weeks — that's the whole labor budget of this campaign, roughly ten operator hours. Put them on your actual calendar as recurring events with names: Sends, Logging, Follow-ups, Audit. Not "radio stuff" — named blocks, because a block with a job gets done and a block with a vibe gets postponed. And schedule the Sunday fifteen-minute review for all three weeks right now, including the war-room photo to your accountability human. Twenty-one days from now, your future self should be able to run this campaign entirely by glancing at a phone.

Load four: the reply infrastructure. Sounds corporate; takes ten minutes. A folder or label for the campaign, filters to catch replies into it, and the send log — the CSV from the zip — open, headers checked, ready to receive row one. Decide now where the log lives and never move it: one file, one truth. The log is both your memory and your Module 06 evidence, and a campaign without one is a rumor you tell yourself later.

Load five: the humans. Your accountability human gets the oath, the war-room photo schedule, and one instruction: "Ask me on Sundays for the photo; if I dodge twice, say the words 'burned well.'" And if you have bandmates or a partner, brief them on the next three weeks now — because the calendar blocks are about to become real and the people who love you deserve to know why Tuesday's 6pm is suddenly sacred. Campaigns that survive are campaigns the household can see.

Then, the freeze review. One pass through everything with the checklist in the workbook: twenty queued rows, four files at one link, pitch frozen at its word count, three subject keepers pasted at the war room's top, calendar blocks named and recurring, log open, oath signed and witnessed. In the worked example, Marisol runs this checklist on Sunday night, March 15th. Twenty rows queued. Assets frozen. Blocks named "Vega Sends — Mon/Wed 6pm." Dev has the oath photo. Total prep time across the whole week: about three hours, most of it already spent in Modules 1 through 3. Monday morning, she sends ten emails in forty minutes — because everything those emails needed was decided days ago.

The failure mode of Week Zero is elegant and common: infinite preparation. New logo, redesign the one-sheet again, reorder the war room columns, re-record the hook cut "just to be sure." That's not loading; that's hiding with a checklist. The antidote is the freeze: when the checklist passes, you are done, and done is a real state you can occupy on purpose. A frozen eighty-five-percent asset outperforms a perfect one you'll send never — and radio, I promise you, cannot tell the difference between your frozen edit and the one you'd have polished for a fourth weekend.

One last calibration before Day 1. Look at your calendar blocks and the twenty rows and say the campaign's actual shape out loud: forty-five minutes, four times a week, for three weeks; twenty emails, twelve Touch 2s, nine Touch 3s; expected honest outcome, one to three live threads and a wall full of silence. That's the machine. Small, visible, survivable. The artists who finish campaigns are not the ones with the most fire on Day 1; they're the ones whose Day 12 requires the least willpower. Build the machine so it runs on calendar, not adrenaline — that's what this week bought you.

A word about what Week Zero feels like, because the checklist is the easy half. The hard half is the silence of a loaded system: everything queued, nothing moving, no feedback, no dopamine. Artists fill that silence with tinkering — I gave you the freeze for that — or with premature sends, which is worse, because a Day-minus-3 send is a Day-1 send with worse data and a calendar lie on top. Sit in the loaded stillness. It's the sound of a machine with potential energy, and every hour you don't spend it early is an hour the cadence spends correctly later. Launch is tomorrow's verb.

The drill: complete the Week Zero checklist — war room queued, assets frozen, calendar blocked, log open, humans briefed. Done means you can photograph the whole loaded system in one image: war room, calendar screen, and the one link open. That photo is your Day 0 artifact. Send it to your accountability human tonight. Tomorrow, first blood.
