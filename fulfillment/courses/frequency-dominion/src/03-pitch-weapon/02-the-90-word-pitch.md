# M3L2 — The 90-word pitch

**Module 03 · The Pitch Weapon** · 10 min lesson (1,084 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Write a pitch a director can finish before the kettle boils — six blocks, one ask, one link.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Ninety words. That's the whole budget. Not because directors can't read — because the ones who add independent music read on their phones, between things, and a pitch they can finish before the kettle boils is a pitch that gets finished. Length is not effort. Length is cost. You are paying for their attention with your brevity, and the exchange rate is public: under ninety words gets read; three hundred gets skimmed for a link; six hundred gets archived with a flick of gratitude that it wasn't a PDF.

The good news: ninety words is a form, and forms can be taught. Six blocks. Block one, the name. Their name, spelled right, and their show named in the first sentence if you can manage it. Not "Hi there." Not "Dear MD." A person.

Block two: why this show, this week. One sentence proving you listen. This is the personalization the oath demands and the part blasts can't fake. "Your March 7 local block — that Wells-to-Corbin segue — was perfect Friday radio." You don't need to review their whole career; you need one observed, specific true thing. If you can't find one thing to say about their actual show, you have not earned the send yet — go listen.

Block three: the song in one sentence. Genre, runtime, and what it does for their clock. "Built for a 10pm block: hook at 0:38, cold ending, clean sweep." You're not describing your feelings. You're describing the job the song performs — which is the station-as-customer lesson compressed into one line.

Block four: runtime plus one proof line. One line from Module 01's stack, chosen per recipient — the venue line for community stations, the prior-add line for specialty hosts, the numbers line for committee-minded MDs. One. Two proof lines is a press release.

Block five: one link. A folder with the WAV, the 320 MP3, the 30-second hook, and the one-sheet. Never attachments unless asked, never three links, never "streaming everywhere" as a lifestyle. The link is the product page; make it one click.

Block six: one ask, with an exit. Spin, interview, or feedback — never two, never all three. And the exit is part of the ask: "If it's not a fit, a one-line no is enough." That clause is not weakness. It's the single highest-performing sentence in independent pitching, because it converts your email from a demand into an offer and it tells a busy human exactly how cheap it is to resolve you. Directors forward pitches with clean exits to each other. I've watched it happen.

Now the writing process, because drafting order matters. Write blocks two and six first — the listening sentence and the ask — since they're the two that are recipient-specific. Then three, four, five, then one. Then read the whole thing aloud with a timer. Over ninety? Cut in this order: cut any adjective describing your own music, cut any sentence about your history or journey, cut "I think you'll really love," cut the second proof line, and merge the song sentence with the runtime. What survives is all load-bearing — which is the actual definition of a pitch.

Let me anticipate the protest: "Ninety words can't convey what my music means." Correct, and it doesn't need to — the song conveys what your music means; the pitch's entire job is to get the song heard by the right show this week. Confusing the two is why six-hundred-word pitches exist: the artist is trying to make the email do the art's job. The division of labor is clean. The subject line buys 1.4 seconds, the pitch buys the click, the folder earns the listen, the song earns the add, and Module 05's humanity earns the next one. Five jobs, five weapons, none of them pretending to be the others.

In the worked example, Marisol's pitch to Dara Whitfield runs eighty-eight words and reads aloud in thirty-eight seconds. Read it in the drawer and count the blocks: her name and show, a segue she actually heard, the song as a job ("3:24, built for a 10pm block"), the proof (KCYQ three weeks, Queen City Beat quote), one link, and one ask with the one-line-no exit. No journey. No "since childhood." No "it would mean the world." Eighty-eight words, and it produced an add that changed the trajectory of her spring. The pitch didn't do it alone — the war room chose the door — but the pitch is what walked through it.

Field note on the ask menu, since "feedback" needs defending. Asking a specialist for honest feedback is a legitimate first-campaign move: it's a lower-risk yes than a spin, it starts a conversation, and some percentage of feedback turns into "actually, send me the WAV." But feedback is the ask, once, for your B-list and your coldest warm doors — it is not a subscription to free A&R for your whole catalogue. And if you ask for feedback, you accept the feedback. Arguing with a reply is arson.

One more discipline point, then the drill: freeze the pitch. Version one gets written, cut, read aloud, cut again, and then locked — because Module 04's send days need identical ammunition to produce clean data. You'll write the personal-first-line fresh per recipient, and swap the proof line per format, but the bones stay frozen for the whole campaign. Iteration happens between campaigns, in the Module 06 audit, with numbers. Mid-campaign rewrites are how you lose the ability to know what worked.

Let me also settle the personalization-range question, because it's the one artists over-rotate on. The blocks that change per recipient: the name, the listening sentence, and the proof line. The blocks that stay frozen: the song sentence, the link, and the ask. That ratio — three variable parts on a frozen skeleton — is the maximum personalization that survives twenty-one days of four work-blocks a week. More than that and you're writing twenty bespoke essays, burning out by day six, and your data dies with your stamina. Less than that and you've broken the oath. Three parts fresh, three parts frozen, ninety words, one ask.

The drill: write your ninety-word pitch, all six blocks. Read aloud — if you're over forty seconds or over ninety words, cut in the order I gave you until it fits a kettle boil. Then paste it into the swipe file as version one, frozen. Done means one artifact exists: a sub-ninety-word pitch you would send to your number-one A-list target today.

## Drill — done when

Write the 90-word pitch. Read it aloud. Cut until it's under 90 words / 40 seconds. Freeze as v1.

**Done when:** Done = one pitch, ≤90 words, six blocks present, pasted into the swipe file as v1. Paste your word count next to it.

### Template

```
Hi [Name] —
[One true sentence proving you listen to their show.] I’m [Artist], [city] [genre]. “[Song]” is [runtime], built for [daypart]: [hook timing / cold ending / sweep]. I [local date/venue] on [date]. Proof: [one true line]. Radio edit: [one link].

One ask: [spin on show before date]? If it’s not a fit, a one-line no is enough.

— [Artist] · [City] · [phone]
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Two asks in one pitch — “a spin, and maybe an interview, and feedback on the bridge?” — which reads as zero asks.

**Fix:** One ask per pitch, chosen for the recipient's format, always with the one-line-no exit. If you want the interview too, that's the Touch 2 or the call — a different day, a different door.

**Subject:** Cincinnati midnight-synth for The Night Signal — 3:24

— Marisol Vega · Cincinnati · (513) 555-0184

Word count: 88/90. Read aloud in 38 seconds.
