# M1L2 — Station as customer

**Module 01 · Command Identity** · 11 min lesson (1,126 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Map what a music director actually buys with a spin — then make your track fit the receipt.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Time to kill the most expensive sentence in independent music: "If they just heard it, they'd get it."

They won't just hear it. That's not cynicism, it's inventory math. A radio spin is not a gift a kind adult bestows on a deserving artist. A spin is sixty to two hundred and forty seconds of finite, scheduled, measured inventory — inventory the station's listeners, advertisers, and branding all lean on. When a music director adds your song, they are not rewarding your effort. They are buying something. Your entire campaign — the Vault, the war room, the pitch — works only after you can say out loud what they're buying.

So let's itemize the purchase. Constraint one: the clock. Every format has a ceiling — three-thirty in tight commercial and college rotation blocks, looser in specialty shows, and it's never about how good your seven-minute progressive suite is. If the SKU doesn't fit the shelf, the shelf doesn't care how it was made. Constraint two: format. A college AAA add moves differently than a community station's local hour, a specialty show at 10pm, or a commercial playlist committee that meets Tuesdays. Same song, four different purchases. Constraint three: legal and standards. Clean lyrics, clean metadata, no hidden profanity in the outro — because one FCC headache erases a hundred goodwill credits. Constraint four: localism. This is your structural advantage as an independent: a station sounds more planted, more of its city, when it plays its city. Localism is the discount coupon you were born with. Constraint five: effort cost. If playing you requires a download, a login, a WeTransfer expiry, or a twenty-minute hunt for a clean edit, you are charging the station labor. Labor is a price. Directors pay it only for people who make it effortless.

Constraint six is the one nobody lists, and it's the realest: ego and identity. A host who spins you is quietly saying "I found this" to their audience. They buy the story they get to tell on air. Give them one sentence of it — where you're from, what the song's about, why tonight — and you've lowered their cost again.

Now the exercise. Pick the one format you're actually targeting first — not five formats, one. In the workbook you'll write five constraints for that format: the clock max, the localism hook you own, one competing current your song sits next to, and why this week specifically a spin makes sense. Then the part that separates this course from a pep talk: cut or edit one song to fit the tightest clock on that list.

In the worked example, Marisol's "Static Bloom" is four forty-one with a fifty-six second intro on the album. Dead on arrival for a three-thirty specialty block. So the radio edit is not a compromise — it's a product. Intro crushed to twelve seconds. Verse two halved. The fade replaced with a hard stop, because a cold ending sweeps clean into imaging and hosts love a sweep that behaves. Three twenty-four, six seconds of air under the ceiling. That edit is one of the highest-leverage forty minutes she spends in the entire campaign, and it happened before a single email went out.

While you're in the workbook, do the lyric pass now, not after someone complains. Flip or soften anything that would force a director to check the safe-hours clock. Decide your clean edit on purpose. And write your metadata like it will be read on air, because it will: artist, title, runtime, ISRC if you have it, one contact email. That's not admin. That's the packaging on the product you're selling.

Here's the mindset shift to carry out of this lesson. Stop asking "how do I get added" and start asking "what does this specific show buy when it plays me, and have I made that purchase cheap?" Clock, format, standards, localism, effort, story. Six prices on one receipt. Your song plus your pitch pays all six or the sale doesn't close.

Let me make the six constraints concrete with the war-room lens you'll build next module, because "pick one format" is where most artists freeze. Your format ladder, from warmest to coldest door: specialty shows first — the 10pm genre blocks where a host personally chooses every song and one email from a local can genuinely land. Community stations second — local hours, live sessions, the most localism per watt on the dial. College rotation third — committee adds, semester rhythms, chart reporting. Commercial specialty and local hours fourth — tighter clocks, more politics, but a real badge when it hits. Full commercial rotation: not a first campaign. Not because you can't — because the purchase price in proof is higher than a first campaign can pay, and pretending otherwise wastes the sends. Marisol's campaign touches rungs one, two, and three only. Twenty emails. That's the ladder.

Now, how the format changes what the same song buys. When a specialty host spins "Static Bloom," they bought twelve minutes of "I found this" credibility with their night audience. When a community station's local hour adds it, they bought proof the station sounds like its own city. When a college MD adds it to rotation, they bought a current that sits between the dark-synth adds they already run. Three different receipts, same track — which is why your war room marks the format on every row, and why your pitch's second sentence changes per rung even when the song doesn't.

One more reframe and it's the one veterans nod at. The clock isn't your enemy; it's the most honest A&R in the business. A three-thirty ceiling is a machine that tells you the truth about your song's architecture: where the hook is, whether the intro earns its length, whether the ending respects the next song. Artists pay consultants thousands for that feedback. The clock gives it free, every edit. When Marisol cut "Static Bloom" to 3:24, she discovered the hook was strong at 0:38 but the second pre-chorus was decoration — information she then used on the next record. Treat the edit as market research you get to keep.

And a practical pass to fold into the drill: after the edit, listen to your radio cut once on the worst speaker you own — phone face-up on a table, laptop speakers, car at highway volume. Radio is not a listening room. If the hook doesn't arrive on a phone face-up on a table, it doesn't arrive. Fix the mix or fix the expectation, but know which one you're shipping.

The drill today: one format, five written constraints, one edit or cut. The edit is the drill. A sentence in a workbook is understanding; a three twenty-four that fits a three thirty is command.

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Sending the 7:00 album mix “because the intro is the vibe” to a 3:30-clock show — then blaming the industry.

**Fix:** Build the radio edit as its own product: kill the intro, halve a verse, end cold. If the song can't survive a tight edit, pitch it to specialty-only shows that run long, and say the runtime in the subject line.

## Worked example — Marisol Vega

### The clock page — 4:41 album cut → 3:24 radio edit

**Problem:** Album cut of ‘Static Bloom’ is 4:41 with a 56-second intro. Dead on arrival for a 3:30 clock.

**Edits made:**

- Intro 0:56 → 0:12 (ambient pad swapped for the arp that was at 1:40)

- Verse 2 halved; second pre-chorus dropped

- Outro fade (0:22) replaced with a hard stop — cold ending sweeps clean

- Final runtime 3:24 — fits a 3:30 clock with 6 seconds of air

**Constraint list for the target format:**

- Clock max: 3:30 (specialty blocks) — the edit is 3:24

- Clean lyrics (FCC safe-hours thinking): ‘hell of a night’ kept, one ‘goddamn’ flipped to ‘damn the night’ — decided before anyone asked

- Cold ending for imaging sweeps — no fade

- Localism hook: Cincinnati named in verse 1 (‘the Elm Street loop’) — say-on-air ammo

- Competing current: sits between dark-synth adds and 80s-resurgence blocks, not against top-40

- Why this week: hometown ticketed date Apr 17 — a reason to spin now, not someday
