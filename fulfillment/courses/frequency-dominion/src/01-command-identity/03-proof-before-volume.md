# M1L3 — Proof before volume

**Module 01 · Command Identity** · 9 min lesson (1,085 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Assemble a 4-line credibility stack that is entirely true — and schedule the week that fills the gaps.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Here is what happens to your pitch the moment it lands. A director — or more often a specialty host doing this at 11pm on a weeknight — gives you roughly one to four seconds of thumb. In that window they are not asking "is this good?" They're asking three cheaper questions: is this for my show, is this person real, and has anyone else vouched for them. Question three is proof, and proof is the difference between an open and a scroll-past.

So we build a proof stack. Four lines. Each line is one verifiable fact that answers "why should a stranger in radio risk sixty seconds of inventory on you." And the rule that governs every line: true, verifiable, specific. Not "generating buzz." Not "blowing up locally." A line that a director can check in one click or take on your word because it's boring. Boring is credible. Hype is a scroll-past.

Line one is almost always a local show date. Venue, capacity, city, date, ticketed or not. Why does this lead? Because a date is a test another operator already ran. Somebody booked you, a room bet on you, a calendar believes in you. It also gives the station the localism discount we covered — a spin now ties to an event their audience can walk to.

Line two is prior airplay, if you have any, at any scale. A three-week run on one specialty show five states away is a real line. "Some college stations played my last single" is not — no station, no show, no duration, no verification. If you have zero airplay, line two becomes the strongest adjacent fact you own: a playlist add with curator name, a support slot for a touring act, a festival slot, a sync placement, a notable blog premiere with the outlet named.

Line three is press — but under eight words. You get one quote, one outlet. "Static-charged pop that earns its neon — Queen City Beat." That's a line. Two paragraphs of your bio is not a line, it's a toll booth. If no press exists, line three is numbers, honestly stated: monthly listeners, the regional percentage, mailing list size. Small numbers stated precisely beat big numbers implied. 2,400 monthly listeners with 61% in Ohio tells a Cincinnati host exactly what kind of local gravity they're buying. "Growing fast!" tells them nothing and smells like everything.

Line four is a champion: a named human at another station, venue, or outlet who would answer an email about you. If you have one, name them in the pitch ("Elena at KCYQ ran my last single three weeks"). If you don't, line four is the thing you're doing to get one — and that's the second half of this lesson.

Because maybe you're watching this with an empty stack. No add, no press, no champion. Good — now you know exactly what Week -1 is for. You manufacture ethical proof. One hometown gig at the smallest real venue that will have you. One interview at the smallest community station that answers its email. One premiere at a local blog with forty readers. These are not beneath you; they are the first rungs of a ladder that never gets shorter. Dominion is earned in public, then leveraged in the inbox — in that order, every time.

What you never do is rent fake proof. Bought playlist adds, invented chart placements, "as heard on" a station that never spun you. Beyond the ethics — and it is theft from your own future — it's verifiable in one click, and the directors who catch you talk to each other. This course's whole architecture is that you become easy to verify. Fake proof is a wire cut in that architecture.

The drill: write your four lines in the workbook. Where a line is empty, don't leave it blank — schedule the action that fills it within seven days, with a date, and put that date in your calendar before you close the workbook. An empty line with a plan is a campaign. An empty line with a shrug is the unheard tax, compounding.

Your stack will be modest. Marisol's is modest: one 240-cap hometown date, one specialty run in Columbus, one blog quote, 2,400 listeners. Modest is fine. Modest and specific and true outperforms loud and fogged, every single send.

Before I let you go, two field notes on using the stack, because writing it and deploying it are different muscles. Field note one: the stack is modular, not monolithic. All four lines never go in one email — the ninety-word pitch has room for exactly one, and Module 03 teaches you to choose per recipient. Dara at WVMR gets the prior-add line, because specialty hosts respect other specialty hosts. A community station's local hour gets the venue line, because localism is their whole religion. A college MD gets the numbers line, because committees want evidence of traction. Same stack, different receipts — you'll practice the selection in the pitch lesson.

Field note two: proof has a half-life, and it's shorter than you think. A show date is proof until the day after the show — then it needs replacing, which is why your calendar in Module 06 staggers dates you can point at. A specialty run is proof for roughly a season. A quote is proof for as long as the outlet stays online. This decay is not a problem; it's the engine. It's why dominion is a loop and not a purchase: each cycle refreshes the stack, and the stack is what you spend next cycle. In the worked example, the fall campaign's KCYQ line is exactly what makes the March campaign's pitch land. One true line, spent twice, compounding.

And if you truly have nothing yet — no date, no add, no quote, no champion, numbers in the low double digits — hear this clearly: you are not disqualified, you are at the start of the ladder, and the ladder's first rung is closer than you think. The smallest venue that answers email. The community station that takes walk-in interviews. The blog with forty readers. Book one, do one, pitch one — seven days, three actions, and your stack has its first true line. That's not a consolation prize; that's precisely how every stack in this course got built, including the example you're holding. The artists you envy have four lines too. They just started theirs earlier. Start yours this week — dated, calendared, real.

## Drill — done when

Write the 4-line proof stack. Any empty line gets a dated action inside 7 days.

**Done when:** Done = 4 written lines (true ones), and every originally-empty line has a calendar entry with a date. Paste the stack into your notes.

### Template

```
1. Local date: ____
2. Prior airplay / strongest adjacent fact: ____
3. Press quote (≤8 words) or honest numbers: ____
4. Named champion (or the dated plan to earn one): ____
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Padding the stack with vibes — “creating a buzz,” “blowing up,” paid playlist numbers — because four true lines feel small.

**Fix:** Small and specific wins: venue + cap + date, station + show + weeks, quote + outlet ≤8 words, listeners + regional %. If a line is empty, schedule the real-world action that fills it — don't decorate the gap.
