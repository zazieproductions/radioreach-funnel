# M1L1 — The unheard tax

**Module 01 · Command Identity** · 8 min lesson (1,174 words of spoken script ≈ 9 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Name the exact cost of another ignored release cycle — and the sentence that ends it.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Let's start with what this actually costs you, because nobody does this math and it's the only math that matters.

You release a record. You post it. Maybe three hundred people hear it, and two hundred and forty of them already liked you. Six weeks later you release another one. Same six weeks of your life. Same result. That gap — between the record you made and the number of humans who heard it — is a tax. The unheard tax. You pay it every single cycle, and unlike most taxes, you can zero it out. But not with talent. Talent was never the line item.

Here's the uncomfortable part. If you have been releasing music for more than a year and radio has never touched it, the problem is almost never the songs. The problem is that no station has a reason to play you that survives contact with a Tuesday. Music directors don't wake up wondering about your dreams. They wake up with a show to fill, a clock to respect, an audience to keep, and about forty emails for every one they can actually read. Your job in Frequency Dominion is to stop asking them to care and start handing them something that works the second they say yes.

That begins with one sentence. Not a bio. Not a brand. One sentence: I am the genre artist city stations use when they need a specific job done on a specific kind of show. Write it with me now. I am the bracket-genre bracket artist, from bracket-city, that stations call on when they need bracket — and here's the trick — the bracket can't say "exposure" or "a chance." It has to name a listener feeling or a programming job. A Friday-night mood. A local hour that sounds planted. A 3:24 with a cold ending that sweeps clean into the top of the hour.

Why one sentence? Because every asset in this course is generated from it. Your subject lines are the sentence compressed to eight words. Your ninety-word pitch is the sentence expanded with proof. Your war room is the sentence turned into a map — the stations the sentence is true for. If the sentence is vague, everything downstream of it is vague, and vague is the tax.

Now the insult test, and I mean that word. Read your sentence and ask: could this fit any other artist in any other city? If yes, it's not a sentence, it's a costume. "Singer-songwriter chasing dreams" fits eighty thousand people and helps zero of them. "The alt-country artist Asheville community stations use when they need a 2:58 night-drive local with a live date this month" fits exactly one human on earth, and that human gets opened.

In the worked example that ships with this course, Marisol Vega writes five drafts. Draft one is dead on arrival — "I'm a synth-pop artist from Cincinnati chasing my dream." Draft five survives: "I'm the midnight-synth artist Cincinnati specialty shows call when they need a local 3:24 that sounds like rain-slick 1987 Berlin and lands its hook before the second chorus." Notice what's in there: genre precise enough for on-air patter, city, clock, structure, and a simile a host can steal. That sentence is a tool other people can use to program you. That's the whole game.

Open the workbook. Write five drafts. Don't polish draft one; drafts two through four exist to be wrong in useful ways. Circle the survivor. Pin it above your send list — literally, print it, tape it to the wall next to the war room you'll build in Module 02. From this moment, every email you send in this course has to be consistent with that sentence. The day the sentence feels small, you don't abandon it, you sharpen it.

One more thing before the drill, and this is the standard I'll hold you to for all six modules. No fake proof, ever. We are not inventing adds. We are not implying chart positions that don't exist. Everything you build in this course is true or it's nothing, because the entire architecture only works when a director can verify you in one click. Truth is not a moral bonus here. Truth is the strategy.

Let me arm you against the two objections that kill this exercise, because they arrive within ten minutes, guaranteed. Objection one: "My music crosses genres — I don't want to box myself in." You are not boxing yourself in. You are choosing a door. A director cannot file "genre-fluid eclecticism" anywhere, which means they file it in the trash. Pick the genre door for the campaign — the sentence targets a show, not your soul. When the campaign works, you'll have proof to walk through the next door with. Marisol writes dark synth-pop with jazz voicings and a Spanish-language bridge. None of that is in the sentence, because none of it gets a song added on a Friday-night block. The sentence carries the campaign; the song carries the range.

Objection two: "There are no stations in my kind of town." Wrong scale of thinking. The sentence doesn't require a city of millions — it requires a you and a market. If you're in a town of eight thousand, your sentence names the region and the college station forty minutes away that plays exactly one local hour a week. That hour is a real door, it is less contested than any metro frequency, and the Vault has it indexed. Small pond, real fish, verified in Module 02. The sentence is about precision, not population.

And a word on what this sentence is not, because artists smuggle resumes into it. It is not your influences. It is not your mission. It is not what you hope critics will someday say. It is a tool a stranger can use to program you, built out of four load-bearing parts: genre they can say on air in four words, city or region, the show-job your song performs, and one structural fact that proves you know what a clock is. When a host reads Marisol's sentence, they don't have to translate anything — it arrives pre-translated into radio. That's the standard. Pre-translated into radio.

Last thing. Write the sentence somewhere permanent, because you're going to use it more than any lyric you've ever written. It goes in your email signature for this campaign. It's the first line of your EPK in Module 03. It's how you introduce yourself on a twenty-second call in Module 05. It's what you say at the merch table when the opening DJ asks what you sound like. One sentence, used consistently, across twenty-one days, starts to become what stations actually know you as. That's identity. That's command. You don't get it by being mysterious. You get it by being specific, out loud, on schedule.

Today's drill is one artifact and one artifact only: the identity sentence, written, insult-tested, pinned. Don't pitch anyone. Don't open the Vault yet. The sentence is the keel. Everything else is the ship.

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** The sentence stays generic — “passionate artist with a unique sound” — because specifics feel risky.

**Fix:** Name the daypart and the job. If nothing else survives: “the [genre] artist [city] plays when they need a [runtime] local that [structure fact].” Structure facts (hook timing, cold ending) are specifics nobody else can claim.

## Worked example — Marisol Vega

### Marisol’s identity sentence — five drafts, one survivor

**Draft 1:** “I’m a synth-pop artist from Cincinnati chasing my dream.”
→ Dead on arrival. Could fit any artist in any city. No station, no job, no clock.

**Draft 2:** “I’m an electronic artist making retro music.”
→ Genre vague, city gone, listener feeling absent. Still a fan’s sentence.

**Draft 3:** “I’m a Cincinnati synth artist for late-night radio.”
→ Closer. ‘Late-night radio’ is a real daypart. Still no runtime, no proof of fit.

**Draft 4:** “I’m the Cincinnati synth artist specialty shows use for Friday 10pm blocks — 3:24, hook at 0:38.”
→ Almost. Daypart, clock, and structure. Missing the emotional cue a host can say on air.

**Draft 5:** “I’m the midnight-synth artist Cincinnati specialty shows call when they need a local 3:24 that sounds like rain-slick 1987 Berlin and lands its hook before the second chorus.”
→ PINNED. Insult test passed: no other artist on earth fits this sentence.

**Pinned above the send list:** “I’m the midnight-synth artist Cincinnati specialty shows call when they need a local 3:24 that sounds like rain-slick 1987 Berlin and lands its hook before the second chorus.”
