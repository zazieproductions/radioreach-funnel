# M2L1 — Hometown heat map

**Module 02 · Target Architecture** · 9 min lesson (1,198 words of spoken script ≈ 9 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Extract every relevant station within 90 minutes of home — ranked, deduped, and named.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Open the Vault. Not metaphorically — pause this and open it, because this lesson is a lab, not a lecture. We are building your hometown heat map, and the hometown is where every campaign in this course begins. Not because it's cute. Because it's the only market where you're not a stranger.

Understand the mechanism first. A director championing a local artist is making their station sound planted — rooted in the soil of its own city. Every market has a handful of humans whose entire on-air identity is "I find the good stuff from here." They are the highest-percentage doors in radio, they are systematically ignored by everybody blasting LA playlists from a bedroom in Belgium, and the Vault indexes them. That's your edge. It is not talent. It is geography, worked on purpose.

The extraction, step by step. Filter one: your metro — every station, every contact, every show. Don't pre-judge formats yet; extraction is not judgment. Filter two: adjacent cities inside a ninety-minute drive — the commute ring, the towns your scene already leaks into. Ninety minutes is the radius where a gig is plausibly "local" to both of you and a drive-time mention actually matters. Filter three, and artists skip this one constantly: specialty shows inside those stations. The 10pm Friday synth block. The Sunday local hour. The Wednesday midnight genre show. Specialty hosts choose every song they play. Rotation committees choose quarterly. You want the individual human with taste and autonomy, and the show field in the Vault is how you find them.

Highlight order, and this order is load-bearing: specialty hosts first, then music directors, then community station programmers, then — and only then — program directors at bigger signals. A general manager's line goes last or not at all. You are not networking with radio. You are choosing which twenty individual humans get your A-game, and this is the ranking of who can actually say yes.

Target count: twenty-five extracted, before scoring. I'll say the number now so it stops being mysterious: twenty-five candidates get scored in the next lesson, twenty go to war, five get cut. That pipeline ratio is not superstition; it's the difference between a chosen list and a grabbed list. Grabbed lists produce "I emailed some stations." Chosen lists produce a campaign you can audit in Module 06.

Dedup against your history before anything else. If you've contacted a station before — last single, last band, three years ago — that's not a fresh target, that's a relationship with a status. The send log from your last cycle decides: warm door (they replied), cold door (they never answered), burned door (you spammed them — Module 05 has the repair, don't send yet). Warm doors get priority in scoring. Burned doors get quarantined. This is exactly why the send log exists from day one: your past sends are map data, and unlogged sends are driving with your eyes closed.

In the worked example, Marisol pulls her Cincinnati metro, then the ninety-minute ring — Covington and Newport across the river, Dayton and Oxford and Hamilton and Middletown up the interstate — then the Columbus, Louisville, Indianapolis ladder for the weekend-run logic you'll get in lesson two. Twenty-five candidates surface. Three die at scoring — an active rock station that's right-city-wrong-planet, a syndicated top-40 that takes no unsolicited music, a standards station full of lovely people who will never play midnight-synth. Two more collapse as duplicates of the same contact listed under two roles. Twenty survive. Every survivor has one thing in common: a named human. No name, no row. That's the extraction's iron rule — an email to "music@" is a prayer, not a pitch.

Now the A/B/C pre-rank, which pre-loads your scoring work. A means personal audio goes to this person — the 30-second hook, the phoner offer, first-name subject line. B means a clean template send, correct and respectful, without the bespoke audio courtesies. C means nurture — quarterly, when there's news, never during this campaign's send windows. You're not judging worthiness; you're allocating your best energy where the fit says it converts.

Two failure modes to name before the drill, because both are seductive. Failure one: extraction sprawl — filtering six states "to be safe" and drowning in four hundred rows. Safety is not coverage; safety is a twenty-row war room you can actually work daily. The Vault will always hold more stations than your campaign can serve. That's what makes it an arsenal instead of a lottery ticket. Failure two: skipping the small signals. The 200-watt community station with twelve listeners-per-watt of local credibility is a better first add than a 50,000-watt you'll never crack. Charts in Module 06 don't care about wattage; they care that a human with a show chose your record.

Let me also give you the search order inside the Vault, because the filter field is only half the skill. After metro and ring, sort by role, not by station size: specialty host and music director rows first, and read the notes column before you read anything else — the Vault's notes are where gold hides, like "runs the local hour," "answers email fast," or "prefers WAV links." Those micro-facts decide your personal first line three weeks from now, and harvesting them during extraction is ten times cheaper than hunting them at send time. Flag any row whose note mentions a policy — "no unsolicited," "submit via form," "quarterly adds only" — because those rows are workable, but only through their stated door, and mis-sending to them burns a contact you could have honored instead.

One more extraction principle: you are building a heat map, not a contact dump, so capture context as you go. Next to each row, one or two words on why the station matters — "only local hour in the metro," "the synth show," "booked my friend's band for a live session." Those fragments are the difference between twenty rows you have feelings about and twenty rows you'll fight for. When day twelve of the campaign is quiet and you're tired, a war room full of "why" notes re-recruits you. Names and numbers alone won't.

And a note on emotional hygiene during extraction, because this is where comparison disease starts. You will see stations in your metro with bigger signals, richer histories, and cooler reputations than anything you've ever touched, and a voice will say "they'd never play me." The extraction phase has no room for that voice — not because it's wrong, but because it's premature. Extraction collects; scoring decides; the oath governs. You'd be amazed how many "they'd never" stations have a 10pm specialty host who is actively starving for a local artist who does it right. The only way to find out which doors are real is to finish the map.

The drill: extract twenty-five hometown candidates from the Vault into the war-room workbook, deduped against your send history, every row carrying a named human, each pre-ranked A, B, or C. Print it when you're done — paper, wall, next lesson. The heat map is not a spreadsheet exercise; it's the physical territory you're about to command, and commanders look at terrain, not tabs.
