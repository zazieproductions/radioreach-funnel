# M2L4 — The 20-station war room

**Module 02 · Target Architecture** · 8 min lesson (1,086 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Lock the final 20 — printed, on the wall, with statuses you update like a chart.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

Twenty stations. Not two hundred. This lesson exists to defend that number, then to make it physical.

Why twenty is the number. Your campaign runs twenty-one days with four forty-five-minute work blocks a week. Per station, the honest labor is: read the show, write the personal line, send, log, two follow-ups, log again, and handle whatever comes back. Call it twenty to thirty minutes of real attention across the campaign per target. Twenty stations is roughly ten hours of operator work — demanding but doable around a job, and doable at quality, which is the part that matters. Forty stations halves the attention per door and kills the personalization that makes any of this work. Two hundred is not a campaign; it's a coping mechanism. Concentration is the discipline. Dominion over twenty doors beats noise at two hundred, every cycle, in every market, and the example campaign is the proof: twenty sends, one add, one interview, one imaging ask, zero burned wells.

So: the war room itself. One table, printed, on a wall you physically sit near. Columns: number, station, city, contact and role, show and format, score, status, next touch, notes. Rows: your twenty survivors. The status column is sacred — queued, sent, opened, replied, added, no, nurture, dead — and you update it daily like a chart, because it is a chart: your chart, of the only radio market that matters this month, which is yours. The next-touch column kills the disease that actually ends indie campaigns — not rejection, but drift. "Who was I supposed to follow up with Thursday?" is a question the wall answers at a glance, so Thursday happens.

The wall, the paper, the printout — yes, really, in 2026, physically. Two reasons. First, visible terrain changes behavior: a war room you walk past nine times a day nags you better than any app notification, and it makes the campaign a fact of your household instead of a tab. Second, separation of powers: your send log is the system of record, the war room is command visibility — like a studio console versus a spreadsheet. You'll also share one photo of it every Sunday with your accountability human, which is the lightest possible enforcement mechanism and nearly unskippable.

Statuses deserve their semantics drilled, because sloppy statuses produce sloppy campaigns. Queued: verified, personal line drafted or planned, ready. Sent: gone, logged with date and subject. Opened: evidence of engagement — a link click, an opening spike — noted without spyware. Replied: a human answered; the reply clock in Module 05 starts today. Added: confirmed in writing, thank-you sent same day. No: clean no, thanked, door status logged, next touch calendared for the next release. Nurture: alive but not this cycle — quarterly news only. Dead: format change, host left, station dark — and dead is information too; the Vault gets re-verified next cycle. Every status but queued is earned, and the wall can't be gamed because the send log underneath it doesn't lie.

In the worked example, the war room is a thing of beauty by day twenty-one and I want you to read it twice — once for outcomes, once for texture. Row one, WVMR, Dara Whitfield, score five: sent day one, added day nine, spin March 27 at 10:42pm, specialty chart number seven for three weeks. Row two, WCBX, Marcus Boone: replied day four, twenty-second call day five, interview day sixteen, aired day twenty-one — the whole Module 05 relationship arc in one row. Row five, WDLX, Sam Okafor: the clean no, thanked same day, "fall" calendared. Row seven, WOKP: opened — link clicked — then silence, which reads "warmed but shy" and gets a different next touch than cold silence would. And fourteen rows of honest, working silence that still produced an add, an interview, and an imaging ask. That's what a healthy war room looks like: mostly silence, two or three live threads, every row true.

The last piece is the cut list, written on the war room itself: the three-plus rows you refused at scoring, with one-line reasons. It stays visible for the whole campaign because it teaches you something every day you glance at it — what restraint looks like, and that the list you didn't send is part of the campaign's discipline, not a failure of nerve.

A note on running the room day to day, because the wall works only if the ritual does. The rhythm is small and daily: after every send or reply, you walk to the wall and move one status with a pen. Ten seconds. No ceremony. What that micro-ritual actually does is keep the campaign inside your body — stations become places, contacts become people, and the twenty-one days stop being an abstraction you'll "get to" and become a board you're visibly playing. Operators who update the wall daily finish campaigns. Operators who batch "admin for Saturday" are describing, with uncanny precision, the campaigns that die on day ten of silence. Board games get played. Spreadsheets get closed.

And one border rule before we arm the weapons: the war room contains stations, not people's private lives. The contact column carries the professional address the Vault verified and the role that human occupies. It never carries a home number, a personal cell harvested from a flyer, or a DM handle they didn't publish for submissions. If the only way to reach a host is their public show DM, that's a 2 in the named-human column, not an invitation to their inbox. The twenty doors you command are professional doors, and keeping them professional is precisely why they open again next cycle.

And when the campaign ends, the wall has one more job: day twenty-one teardown. Every status gets reconciled against the send log, every live thread gets a next-touch date for the next cycle, and the sheet itself comes down — replaced, when market two opens, by the next twenty. Keeping a dead campaign on the wall past its audit is how rooms become shrines. Date the finished sheet, file it with the log, and let the wall mean one thing: the market you're commanding right now.

The drill: build the final twenty. Print. Wall. Update the status column today — all twenty should read queued, and if any row can't honestly read queued, it isn't ready, and fixing that is the drill. Send one photo of the wall to your accountability human. You are now commanding a defined market with a defined force. Module 03 arms the sends.

## On screen

- Open on a printed war-room sheet going up on a studio wall — tape, glare, real.
- The math card: 20 stations × ~25 min attention ≈ 10 hours ≈ 4 × 45-min blocks × 3 weeks.
- Status lifecycle animation: queued → sent → opened → replied → added/nurture/no/dead.
- Worked example: the Day-21 war room panning down rows — WVMR's add, WCBX's arc, WDLX's clean no, 14 honest silences.
- End card: IF YOU CANNOT MANAGE TWENTY, YOU CANNOT MANAGE A CAREER.

## Drill — done when

Finalize the 20, print the war room, put it on the wall, photo it to your accountability human.

**Done when:** Done = a printed war room exists on a wall (photo = proof) with all 20 rows statused ‘queued’ and a cut list written on it.

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** The war room lives in a spreadsheet tab you never reopen — statuses rot, drift takes over, the campaign dies of admin.

**Fix:** Paper on the wall + Sunday photo ritual. The send log stays the system of record; the wall is command visibility. If it isn't visible, it isn't happening.
