# M2L3 — Format fit scoring

**Module 02 · Target Architecture** · 8 min lesson (1,054 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Score every candidate 0–5 so your A-game only reaches rooms that can say yes.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

You now have twenty-five named humans. Some of them are about to waste your best hour and their last patience. Scoring is how you stop that before it starts.

The score is four questions, zero to five total, and it takes ninety seconds per row once you're moving. Genre fit, zero to two: is your song the kind of thing this station already plays? Not "could they stretch to it" — do they play the lane? Midnight-synth at a modern-rock college block is a two. Midnight-synth at active rock is a zero, and no amount of belief changes it. Locality, zero to one: are you inside their listener map? Same metro is a one; three states away with no date booked is a zero — fix it by booking a date, not by hoping. Named human, zero to one: is there a specific person receiving this, with a show and a name? music@ whatever is a zero, and honestly, a zero here usually disqualifies the row regardless of total — remember, no name, no row. Recent independent adds, zero to one: evidence they actually spin independents at this station — chart pages, specialty shows, local hours, the Vault's notes. A station that plays all majors is telling you the truth about itself. Believe stations.

Read the totals like a triage tag. Four to five: send this week, A-game — personal first line, 30-second hook available, phoner offer. These are the ten sends on day one of your campaign. Two to three: send, but template-tight — correct, respectful, correctly formatted, no bespoke courtesies; these are day three's batch. Zero to one: do not touch. Not "save for later" — do not touch. The zero-to-one row is where burned wells come from, because zero-fit sends feel like spam from the recipient's chair no matter how nicely they're written.

Now watch the scoring run on the example, because the numbers do the arguing. WVMR, the Friday 10pm specialty block in Cincinnati: genre two, locality one, named human — Dara, the host — one, recent indie adds — it's literally a specialty show — one. Five. First email of the campaign, and the one that eventually adds "Static Bloom." WDLX, community Dayton: genre two, locality one, named human Sam one, indie adds — mixed, call it zero. Four. Send, and in fact Sam answers with the campaign's cleanest no — "try me in the fall" — which is a door held open, not a loss. And WKQN, active rock Louisville: genre zero, locality one, name zero, adds zero. One. Right city, wrong planet. It gets cut at scoring, and cutting it is not defeat; it's the discipline that keeps twenty sends dangerous instead of twenty-four sends diffuse.

Here's what scoring really does, and it's bigger than sorting. It converts "radio rejected me" into "this specific cell scored low, and here's which lever moves it." No named human? Go find the show host via the station page — Vault first, station site second, never a data broker. No locality? Book a date in that city and the row re-scores itself. Genre a zero? That station was never your market — stop forwarding it your heart. The score is a diagnostic panel for your whole campaign, updated as facts change. Scores are alive: when you book Columbus, every Columbus row gains a locality point. When KCYQ's host answers, warm-door rows get worked first.

One more thing scoring protects you from: the tyranny of the dream target. Every artist has one — the big signal, the taste-maker station, the one that would change everything. Scoring doesn't tell you to abandon it. It tells you what the dream costs: if it scores a two today, the honest paths are (a) raise the score — book their city, get the smaller station down the road to add you first, build the proof line — or (b) accept a template-tight send with modest expectations. What scoring forbids is spending your A-game, your 30-second hook, your phoner offer — the assets that make day one lethal — on a row that mathematically cannot convert. A-game assets go to 4s and 5s. That's the whole allocation policy.

And the reverse error, just as expensive: under-scoring a row because the station is small. Wattage is not score. A 250-watt community station that plays locals weekly, has a named host who answers email, and sits inside your metro is a five — a genre two, locality one, human one, adds one five. A 50,000-watt commercial that last added an independent during the previous administration is a one. Charts, hosts, and the local scene all know the difference. Score the door, not the tower.

Let me close the loop on what a scored sheet does to your psychology, because it's the least discussed benefit. An unscored list is an emotional document — every row hums with hope and dread in equal measure, and every silence feels like a referendum on your worth. A scored list is an engineering document. When a 5 goes quiet, you investigate: wrong subject? wrong day? host on vacation? — because 5s warrant diagnosis. When a 2 goes quiet, that's not a mystery; that's a 2 behaving as designed, and your attention belongs on the 5s. The score tells you where disappointment is information and where it's just math. Over twenty-one days, that distinction is the difference between finishing the campaign and flinching out of it somewhere around day nine.

Calibration insurance, because scorers drift: rescore any row you feel strongly about, using only the four questions, out loud, with a witness if you have one. Feeling is not a fifth category. The four cells are the whole instrument. If you catch yourself arguing with your own score, that's the dream-target error wearing a disguise — write the lever in the notes column instead of fudging the number.

The drill: score all twenty-five rows from lesson one. Move the four-to-fives to the top of the war room — they're your day-one batch. Mark the zero-to-ones CUT and write one line on why. Then, before you close the sheet, pick the single lowest-scoring row you were emotionally attached to, and write next to it the exact lever that would raise its score a point. That last sentence is the difference between a list and a strategy.

## On screen

- The four scoring dials animate: GENRE 0–2 · LOCALITY 0–1 · NAMED HUMAN 0–1 · INDIE ADDS 0–1.
- Worked example: three rows score on screen — WVMR hits 5, WDLX 4, WKQN 1 → struck through.
- Triage bands card: 4–5 A-GAME · 2–3 TEMPLATE · 0–1 DO NOT TOUCH.
- Diagnostic panel: 'no locality? book a date — the row re-scores itself.'
- End card: SCORE THE DOOR, NOT THE TOWER.

## Drill — done when

Score all 25 rows. 4–5s to the top (day-one batch). CUT the 0–1s with a one-line reason.

**Done when:** Done = every row has a 0–5 score and the sheet is sorted, with at least two rows marked CUT + reason. Filled WB02 screenshot.

### Template

```
Score = genre (0–2) + locality (0–1) + named human (0–1) + recent indie adds (0–1)
4–5 → send this week · 2–3 → template-tight · 0–1 → do not touch
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** Spending the A-game on the dream station that scores 2 — heart over math, every asset wasted on a door that can't open yet.

**Fix:** Either raise the score (book their city, build the smaller add first) or send template-tight and move on. A-game goes to 4s and 5s, full stop.
