# M2L2 — Tour-route pre-roll

**Module 02 · Target Architecture** · 9 min lesson (1,125 words of spoken script ≈ 8 min at teleprompter pace)

**Outcome:** Time pitches 21 days ahead of every date on your calendar — even the calendar you had to invent.

---

## Voiceover script (read this one — teleprompter / VO)

A show without radio is a secret. Radio without a show is a rumor. Stack them and you get the single most powerful timing pattern in regional touring: the 21-day pre-roll.

Here's the mechanism. When a station adds your song three weeks before you play their city, two machines synchronize. The song has time to cycle — specialty shows are weekly, so twenty-one days is three airings, enough for a host's audience to hear you twice and think "again" the third time. And your date has time to ride the spin — every airing between add-day and show-day is a reminder with a ticket attached. A spin the week after your date is a nice memory. A spin three weeks before your date is a funnel. You want the funnel.

Twenty-one days also matches the inbox physics. Send earlier and you're filed and forgotten — directors live in a one-to-two-week decision window. Send later and you've spent all your runway: no time for the three-touch cadence from Module 04, no time for an interview to air before load-in. D-minus-21 isn't folklore. It's the intersection of programming lead time and follow-up math.

So the build. List every real date you have on the books — even one. For each city, we already know the move from the heat map: pull that city's stations from the Vault, specialty first, and mark the send day as date-minus-21 on a literal calendar. Subject line references the venue and the date, because a local date is the strongest "why this week" hook a regional pitch can carry. The body offers an interview window the afternoon of load-in — hosts love same-day phoners with a live event to talk about, and an artist with a date tonight is a story; an artist with a date in five weeks is a plea.

Now the clause that makes this lesson work for everyone watching who has zero dates: if you have no tour, you invent one. Four cities, one weekend, small real rooms, booked by you. This isn't a visualization exercise. Book it — the smallest ticketed room in your city that will have you on a Friday, a neighboring city Saturday, a third Sunday, and if your legs allow, an off-night acoustic somewhere on the drive home. Your hometown headline becomes the proof line; the run becomes the pitch; radio becomes the reason each room isn't empty. That's the flywheel, and it starts with one weekend of nerve.

In the worked example, Marisol has exactly one real date — Friday, April 17, Foundry on Elm, 240-cap, ticketed, her third Cincinnati headline attempt. That's the anchor. She invents the rest of the run around it: Columbus Saturday at a 150-cap room, Louisville Sunday at a 120-cap listening room, Dayton Monday off-night basement. Four cities, four radio markets, one weekend. Then the calendar goes backwards: Columbus sends go out March 28 for the April 18 date; Louisville March 29 for the 19th; Dayton March 30 for the 20th. Her hometown sends are already running inside the 21-day campaign you'll watch unfold in Module 04, so the run doesn't replace the war room — it extends it into three neighboring markets, each with its own D-minus-21 lane.

Look at what that structure does to her pitches. Every subject line in the run has a built-in reason-this-week. Every email has a real event to offer an interview around. Every host who adds gets a station-sponsored ticket night they can promote. And after the weekend, every market has a logged send, a status, and a next touch — the run feeds the loop instead of dying on Monday. Regional acts look national before they are national by exactly this mechanism, run after run, market after market, ledger line after ledger line.

Field notes from operators who run this pattern constantly. Note one: respect the off-night. A Monday show is a hard sell for a station to plug — so pitch Monday cities differently: the in-studio acoustic, the "next time through on a Friday" relationship play. Not every date earns the same ask. Note two: the interview window offer is D-minus-2 to D-minus-1 — the afternoon of load-in — and you offer two slots max, not a scheduling buffet. Busy people respect bounded asks. Note three: never let the radio plan delay the booking. Book the rooms first; radio follows dates, not the reverse. You cannot pre-roll a show that doesn't exist.

And the anti-pattern, named bluntly: the "we're coming through town next week!" email. Sent D-minus-6, it tells the host you planned nothing, offers no time for a spin to cycle, and converts at approximately the rate of a billboard facing the wrong way. If you're inside D-minus-10 for a market, skip the add pitch entirely and go for the Hail Mary that occasionally works: the same-day phoner offer for a local host who likes live events. One ask, honest timing, zero guilt.

Two operator details that make pre-roll feel professional instead of desperate. First: the subject line for route cities carries the venue and the date — "Columbus stop Apr 18 + 'Static Bloom' 3:24" — because for a host in that city, your arrival is the news; the song is the reason to care about the arrival. Reverse that order — song first, date as postscript — and you sound like every other pitch. Second: stagger your sends so your own campaign doesn't collide with itself. Four cities sending on four consecutive days keeps your reply-handling humane; four cities sending the same morning turns a good week into an unmanageable one. The route planner exists to sequence you, not just remind you.

And a word about the rooms themselves, since we're telling the truth in this course: radio support amplifies what's already a workable show. A 240-cap room with three weeks of specialty spins behind it can genuinely move tickets. A 500-cap room with the same support still embarrasses everyone. Book rooms one honest size smaller than your bravado suggests — the run's job is to sell out, get photographed, and generate the ledger lines that make the next run bigger. Marisol's weekend runs 240, 150, 120, 90 in that order, and every room is winnable. That's not timidity; that's compounding. Foundry on Elm at 240 sold out with a charting local single becomes a 400-cap conversation in June with receipts attached.

The drill: map your real dates — or invent and book the four-city weekend — into the route planner, with a D-minus-21 send day, an interview offer, and follow-up days for each city. Print it and pin it next to the war room. The route is your campaign's spine; the war room is the muscle. Next lesson, we score who actually gets worked.

## Drill — done when

Map real dates — or book the invented 4-city weekend — onto D-21 send days in the route planner.

**Done when:** Done = every date has a city row with send day (D-21), interview offer, and two follow-up days written. Screenshot the planner.

### Template

```
City | Date | Station | Contact | Send day (D-21) | Interview offer | Follow-up D-10 | Follow-up D-3
```

## Common failure → fix

**Fails:** The D-minus-6 “we’re coming through next week!” email — no cycling time, no respect signal, dead on arrival.

**Fix:** If you're inside D-10, drop the add pitch; offer the same-day phoner to a local-events host instead. Otherwise calendar D-21 and wait for the next date.
